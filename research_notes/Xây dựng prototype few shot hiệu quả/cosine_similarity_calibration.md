# Cosine Similarity's Narrow Dynamic Range for Frozen ViT/CLIP/DINO Embeddings: Causes and Calibration Techniques

## Is there documented literature explaining WHY frozen ViT/CLIP/DINO embeddings produce a narrow, high-baseline cosine similarity range even between unrelated images ("cone effect" / anisotropy)?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is a well-documented, architecture-level phenomenon (not a training artifact specific to any one model). Deep encoders with non-linear activations provably concentrate their output embeddings into a narrow cone rather than spreading across the full hypersphere, so almost all pairwise cosine similarities — related or unrelated — land in a compressed, elevated band. This directly explains why true-positive and false-positive similarities can be only ~0.1 apart in absolute terms.

### Cited Findings
- The "representation degeneration problem": when training with likelihood maximization and weight tying, learned embeddings collapse into a narrow cone, limiting representational power; most vectors point in "virtually identical directions." — [Gao et al., "Representation Degeneration Problem in Training Natural Language Generation Models," arXiv:1907.12009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.12009)
- Manifestations of representation degeneration/anisotropy are observed broadly: sequential recommendation, language generation, transformers, VAEs, vector quantization, graph neural networks, deep RL. — [Representation Degeneration in ML — Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/representation-degeneration-problem)
- Mean-pooled BERT-style embeddings show mean pairwise cosine similarity around 0.99 in anisotropic settings; when representations are anisotropic, cosine similarity "loses discriminative power" and reports high similarity for all pairs regardless of semantic content. — [Anisotropy in Embedding Representations — Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/anisotropy-in-embedding-representations); [Anisotropy Decides Cosine vs. Rank Metrics for Text Embeddings, arXiv:2606.29571](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29571)
- For CLIP's 512-d image/text representations, Liang et al. measured average cosine similarity of 0.56 — corresponding to occupying less than 1/2^512 of the hypersphere surface, i.e., an extraordinarily narrow cone. — [Liang et al., "Mind the Gap: Understanding the Modality Gap in Multi-modal Contrastive Representation Learning," NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2203.02053](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.02053)
- Mechanistic cause: Theorem 1 in the same paper proves that each network layer with ReLU-type non-linearity probabilistically *increases* cosine similarity between any pair of vectors; MLPs without non-linear activations show "little cone effect," but with non-linearity, average cosine similarity rises rapidly with depth (e.g., reaching 0.99 after just two Sigmoid layers). — [Liang et al., NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2203.02053](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.02053)
- Critically, the cone effect appears even in **randomly initialized, untrained networks fed random noise** (cosine similarity ≈ 0.99), showing this is a structural property of deep non-linear architecture, not something learned from data or specific to any one loss/encoder. — [Liang et al., NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2203.02053](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.02053)
- Because each encoder (modality- or domain-specific) forms its own distinct cone at initialization, and contrastive training does not fully merge cones, a persistent gap/compression is baked in before any fine-tuning; this generalizes beyond text/image to other paired domains, e.g. medical vision-language embeddings. — [Liang et al., arXiv:2203.02053](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.02053); ["On the Cone Effect and Modality Gap in Medical Vision–Language Embeddings," arXiv:2603.17246](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17246v2)
- Frozen CLIP/DINOv2 embeddings are "incentivized in their ambient space to be closer based on cosine similarity if their features are similar," but cosine similarity "only measures the angle between vectors, ignoring magnitude entirely" — two vectors can have very different scales/confidence but the same direction, adding another source of compressed, ambiguous scoring. A magnitude-aware metric ("DNorm") was found to provide *larger* advantages over plain cosine specifically on DINOv2 versus CLIP, suggesting DINO-family embeddings are, if anything, more prone to this geometry issue than CLIP. — ["When Does Embedding Magnitude Matter? A Cross-Task Functional-Symmetry Framework," arXiv:2602.09229](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.09229)
- DINOv2's own training recipe (Sinkhorn-Knopp centering plus a KoLeo regularizer) was explicitly designed to push features toward a more uniform spread and mitigate collapse — an implicit acknowledgment by DINOv2's authors that self-supervised ViT features are prone to anisotropic collapse absent such regularization. — [DINOv2: Learning Robust Visual Features without Supervision, arXiv:2304.07193](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07193v2)

### Inferences
- The project's empirical finding — TP cosine ~0.20–0.34 vs FP ~0.06–0.26 — is consistent with a moderate/partial cone effect: DINOv2 is explicitly regularized against full collapse (unlike raw BERT's ~0.99 pairwise similarity), so its baseline similarity floor is lower and more spread than degenerate NLP embeddings, but the compressed dynamic-range phenomenon documented in the anisotropy/cone-effect literature is the same underlying architectural mechanism.
- The "domain-gap ceiling" the project describes (close-up reference photos vs. drone-video crops) is plausibly an instance of the same geometric phenomenon documented for the CLIP image/text "modality gap": each distinct data sub-manifold/domain a frozen encoder sees (studio close-ups vs. aerial video frames with different scale, motion blur, compression, lighting) can occupy its own semi-separated cone region, adding a domain-specific offset on top of the generic anisotropy compression. This is an inference from the modality-gap mechanism, not a paper that studied drone/close-up domain gap specifically.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that specifically measures anisotropy/cone-effect statistics for DINOv2 or DINOv3 patch/CLS embeddings in a cross-domain (studio-photo vs. drone-video) retrieval setting; the evidence above is generalized from CLIP/BERT/ViT anisotropy literature and general DINOv2 architecture claims, not a DINO-specific cross-domain study.
- Quantitative isotropy metrics (e.g., average pairwise cosine similarity of random DINOv2 embedding pairs) were not directly located for DINOv2/v3 to compare numerically against the project's 0.06–0.34 empirical range.

## What normalization/whitening techniques address this (PCA-whitening, All-but-the-Top, z-score against background, centering)?

### Takeaway
A family of well-established post-processing techniques — mean-centering, PCA/ZCA whitening, and z-score/cohort normalization against a background distribution — directly counteract anisotropy by removing the dominant shared direction(s) and rescaling per-dimension variance, and they reliably restore discriminative power to cosine-similarity-like scores in NLP; the same principle (background/cohort normalization) has a decades-long track record in speaker verification, and is exactly the same family of idea as the project's own RMD.

### Cited Findings
- **All-but-the-Top** (word embeddings): postprocessing that subtracts the mean vector, projects out the top principal component(s), and z-scores each remaining dimension; this shifted the median pairwise cosine similarity of a word-embedding set from 0.833 down to −0.010, i.e., essentially eliminated the anisotropic baseline. — [Mu & Viswanath, "All-but-the-Top: Simple and Effective Postprocessing for Word Representations," arXiv:1702.01417](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.01417)
- **BERT/WhiteningBERT**: applying PCA whitening as a post-hoc step to transformer sentence embeddings significantly improves isotropy and yields an average absolute gain of +4 to +9 Spearman correlation points on semantic textual similarity (STS) benchmarks, precisely because whitening restores separation that cosine similarity could not previously provide on the raw (anisotropic) embeddings. — [WhiteningBERT, arXiv:2104.01767](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.01767); [general whitening summary — Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/whitening-sentence-representations)
- Rationale for why whitening helps: "cosine distance assumes an orthonormal coordinate system, which is not the case for most pre-trained sequence models" — whitening (PCA or ZCA) decorrelates and rescales dimensions so that Euclidean/cosine-style distances become meaningful again. — [WhiteningBERT summary discussion](https://deep-ch.medium.com/dimension-reduction-by-whitening-bert-roberta-5e103093f782)
- Frequency-aware extension: **Zipfian Whitening** shows that standard whitening implicitly assumes uniform token/sample frequency, which is unrealistic; weighting the whitening transform by the empirical (Zipfian) frequency distribution further improves downstream task performance over plain whitening. — ["Zipfian Whitening," arXiv:2411.00680](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00680)
- Whitening has also been adapted specifically for code/embedding search with a **Soft-ZCA** variant, indicating the technique generalizes beyond NLP sentence embeddings to other frozen-embedding retrieval domains. — ["Isotropy Matters: Soft-ZCA Whitening of Embeddings for Semantic Code Search," arXiv:2411.17538](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.17538)
- **Mean-centering for CLIP retrieval**: before mean-centering, CLIP image and text embeddings show high within-modality similarity (~0.3 average) but sit on two separate, non-overlapping cones; after subtracting the (per-modality or joint) dataset mean and re-normalizing, average cosine similarity for image-image, text-text, and image-text pairs becomes ≈0, and the modality gap closes. Mean-centering combined with (approximate) nearest-neighbor search "usually gives a small lift over normalized cosine" in retrieval, especially where embedding-magnitude variance is real. — [Mixpeek, "Embedding Space Geometry: Why Cosine Similarity Doesn't Always Mean What You Think"](https://mixpeek.com/guides/embedding-space-geometry); ["Semantics at an Angle: When Cosine Similarity Works Until It Doesn't," arXiv:2504.16318](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16318v2)
- **Limitation**: whitening (PCA/ZCA/Cholesky variants) is reported as *detrimental for direct downstream classification* in some analyses, with accuracy losses that grow with embedding dimensionality — i.e., whitening is a retrieval/similarity-scoring aid, not a universally free transformation, and should be validated on the target task rather than assumed beneficial. — [search synthesis on PCA/ZCA/Cholesky whitening classification trade-offs](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0306457323000092)
- **Background/cohort score normalization in speaker verification** is a mature, decades-old parallel technique family: Z-norm, T-norm, and the more recent **Adaptive Symmetric Score Normalization (AS-Norm)** all normalize a raw cosine-similarity verification score by the mean/std of similarity scores against a background "cohort" (impostor/negative) population, exactly mirroring z-score-against-background-distribution logic. AS-Norm is standard in state-of-the-art VoxCeleb speaker-verification systems as of 2023. — [DKU-MSXF VoxCeleb Speaker Verification System 2023, arXiv:2308.08766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.08766)
- **Hubness in high-dimensional few-shot embeddings**: a documented related pathology is "hubness," where certain points become spuriously close (high cosine similarity) to many other points in high-dimensional embedding spaces, which especially afflicts few-shot / low-data similarity comparisons; z-score normalization is specifically studied as a mitigation for hubness in the few-shot learning literature. — ["Z-Score Normalization, Hubness, and Few-Shot Learning" (RUC GSAI)](https://gsai.ruc.edu.cn/uploads/20211128/7d943302006b9ff22001734a2b94369e.pdf)

### Inferences
- The project's RMD approach and the general "z-score/whitening against background" literature are the same family of fix: both replace an absolute, geometry-naive score (raw cosine or raw Mahalanobis) with a score *relative to* a fitted background/negative distribution. The speaker-verification AS-Norm precedent is a strong independent validation that this class of fix is an established, production-proven solution to "narrow/overlapping raw-similarity-score" problems in a real biometric open-set matching deployment, not just an OOD-detection theoretical construct.
- Because whitening/All-but-the-Top require fitting a mean vector and (co)variance/PCA basis on a representative background sample, they are inherently a *batch calibration* step — for a streaming drone setting this would need to be fit periodically (e.g., from an accumulating window of background crops) and then applied per-frame as a fixed affine transform; this is compatible with "online" use as long as the whitening parameters are refreshed periodically rather than needing a full closed gallery per query.

### Gaps
- No source was found that specifically evaluates whitening or All-but-the-Top-style postprocessing on frozen DINOv2/DINOv3 visual embeddings in a few-shot prototype-matching or drone-detection context; the evidence is drawn from NLP sentence embeddings, CLIP image/text retrieval, and code-search embeddings, and is extrapolated to the DINO visual-embedding case by analogy.
- No direct quantitative comparison between "whitened cosine similarity" and "RMD" was found in the literature reviewed; whether whitening is redundant with, complementary to, or superior to the project's existing RMD was not established and would need direct experimentation.

## Mahalanobis / relative Mahalanobis distance for OOD detection — how does it compare to cosine similarity for narrow ID/OOD separation, and is this consistent with best practice?

### Takeaway
Ren et al.'s RMD is precisely defined as RMD_k(z) = MD_k(z) − MD_0(z), where MD_0 is the Mahalanobis distance to a single **class-independent (background) Gaussian fit on the entire pooled data** (not per-class); this subtraction cancels out "nuisance" directions that dominate plain Mahalanobis distance in high dimensions and produces large, consistent AUROC gains over plain Mahalanobis across vision, language, and biology near-OOD benchmarks — the project should verify its own implementation fits MD_0 on the full/global background population with its own mean and (shared or global) covariance, not a per-class or per-cluster background.

### Cited Findings
- Exact definition: **RMD_k(z′) = MD_k(z′) − MD_0(z′)**, where MD_k(z′) is the Mahalanobis distance from sample z′ to class/cluster k's fitted Gaussian N(μ_k, Σ_k), and MD_0(z′) is the Mahalanobis distance to a background Gaussian N(μ_0, Σ_0) fit over **all** training data pooled together (μ_0 = mean of all z_i; Σ_0 = covariance of all z_i, ignoring class labels). — [Ren et al., "A Simple Fix to Mahalanobis Distance for Improving Near-OOD Detection," arXiv:2106.09022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.09022)
- Diagnosed failure mode of plain Mahalanobis distance: in high-dimensional feature spaces, low-variance/"nuisance" dimensions can numerically dominate the Mahalanobis score and swamp the few truly discriminative dimensions, causing ID and near-OOD classes to become confused (plain MD reaches only 74.91–74.98% AUROC on CIFAR-100-vs-CIFAR-10 near-OOD). — [Ren et al., arXiv:2106.09022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.09022)
- Why the subtraction works: for non-discriminative/nuisance dimensions, the class-conditional and class-independent (background) likelihoods are approximately equal, so they cancel in the subtraction (RMD acts like a log-likelihood-ratio/relative test); for genuinely discriminative dimensions, the difference remains large. This is analogous in spirit to the project's own reasoning for treating the background/negative covariance structure explicitly rather than scoring in raw feature space. — [Ren et al., arXiv:2106.09022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.09022)
- Quantitative gains reported: CIFAR-100 vs. CIFAR-10 near-OOD AUROC improved from 74.91% (MD) to 81.01% (RMD); Genomics OOD from 53.10% to 68.98%; CLINC intent-detection OOD from 75.48% to 91.98% (with BERT pretraining) — up to a 15-point AUROC gain on the hardest (genomics) near-OOD benchmark. RMD is also reported as more robust to hyperparameter choice than plain MD. — [Ren et al., arXiv:2106.09022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.09022); [abstract, arXiv:2106.09022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09022)
- Follow-up literature exists refining Mahalanobis-based OOD scoring further: a 2025 "geometry-based view of Mahalanobis OOD detection" re-examines Mahalanobis-family scores' theoretical grounding, and a 2025 "dynamic covariance calibration" paper specifically targets weaknesses in how the covariance matrix is estimated/updated for OOD detection — both are candidate directions if the project wants to push RMD further. — ["A Geometry-Based View of Mahalanobis OOD Detection," arXiv:2510.15202](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15202); ["Improving Out-of-Distribution Detection via Dynamic Covariance Calibration," arXiv:2506.09399](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.09399)

### Inferences
- Because RMD's power comes specifically from the *background Gaussian being fit on pooled/global data with its own full covariance structure* (not a diagonal approximation, not per-cluster), the project's implementation should be checked for two common deviations from best practice: (1) whether Σ_0 is a full covariance matrix (capturing correlated "nuisance" directions, which is the entire point of the fix) rather than a diagonal/isotropic approximation, and (2) whether the background population used to fit N(μ_0, Σ_0) is broad/representative of the real negative-confuser distribution seen in drone footage (since MD_0's job is to absorb shared nuisance variation common to both true and false matches).
- The "dynamic covariance calibration" (2025) line of follow-up work is relevant to the project's streaming setting: since drone footage conditions (lighting, altitude, motion blur) drift over a flight, a background covariance Σ_0 estimated once may become stale, and the project's existing "adaptive/online thresholding" could potentially be extended to periodically re-estimate Σ_0 rather than only re-calibrating a scalar threshold.

### Gaps
- The project's actual RMD implementation code was not inspected as part of this web research pass (out of scope — this note is literature-only); a direct code-level comparison against the Ren et al. formula (full-covariance background Gaussian, class-independent) is a recommended follow-up but was not performed here.
- No paper was found applying RMD specifically to frozen DINOv2/DINOv3 visual features for few-shot prototype matching; all quantitative RMD results above are from Ren et al.'s original vision (CIFAR), language (CLINC), and biology (Genomics) benchmarks, not from a visual few-shot retrieval setting resembling aero_eyes.

## Query expansion / re-ranking (k-reciprocal, diffusion re-ranking, database-side augmentation) — applicable to real-time streaming, or do they need a closed/offline gallery?

### Takeaway
Nearly all classical re-ranking techniques from image retrieval and person re-ID (k-reciprocal encoding, diffusion-based re-ranking, database-side feature augmentation) are fundamentally **gallery-side, batch techniques**: they work by exploiting mutual-neighbor structure across a fixed, multi-item candidate set, and their main efficiency trick (precomputing diffusion offline) explicitly assumes the database is static. In aero_eyes's setting — one small reference prototype, frame-by-frame streaming candidate crops, no fixed multi-item gallery — none of these apply directly without a substantial adaptation (e.g., building a rolling small "gallery" of recent high-confidence detections), and even then their benefit is unproven for a single-item-probe scenario.

### Cited Findings
- **k-reciprocal re-ranking** (Zhong et al., CVPR 2017): computes, for each gallery image, its "k-reciprocal nearest neighbors" within the gallery, encodes this into a Jaccard-distance-based re-ranking score; the hypothesis is "if a gallery image is similar to the probe in the k-reciprocal nearest neighbors, it is more likely to be a true match." This requires a multi-item gallery over which mutual-neighbor relationships can be computed — it is not defined for a single probe against a single/few reference images. — [Zhong et al., "Re-ranking Person Re-identification with k-Reciprocal Encoding," CVPR 2017, arXiv:1701.08398](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.08398)
- **Diffusion-based re-ranking**: propagates similarity through a random-walk/graph built over the *complete dataset manifold* (query + gallery), which inherently requires seeing the full candidate set (or at least the gallery) to construct the affinity graph. — [general diffusion retrieval synthesis](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3803010)
- The state-of-the-art efficiency fix explicitly **decouples diffusion into offline (database-side, precomputed once) and online (query-side, reduced to a linear combination) processing** — but this only works because the *database/gallery is fixed and precomputed in advance*; at query time it just becomes a fast lookup against that precomputed structure, achieving ~10x online speedup. This confirms that the heavy/expensive part of diffusion re-ranking fundamentally assumes a closed, static gallery — it is not something that can be done meaningfully on a rolling set of frame-by-frame candidate crops that are seen once and discarded. — [Yang & Hinami, "Efficient Image Retrieval via Decoupling Diffusion into Online and Offline Processing," arXiv:1811.10907](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.10907)
- **Query expansion (QE)**, e.g. Average Query Expansion (AQE): averages the top-k *retrieved gallery* features into a new, enriched query and re-searches. This still assumes a retrieved set of gallery candidates exists to average over — in a single-probe, streaming setting there is no gallery to draw expansion candidates from in the traditional sense; however, the *inverse* direction (expanding the **query/prototype** side using multiple confirmed positive reference crops or high-confidence online detections) is conceptually compatible with streaming and is structurally similar to what "database-side augmentation" does, just applied to the probe/prototype instead of the gallery. — [query expansion synthesis, multiple sources](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~lewms/mir/session2/Semantic%20Concept-Based%20Query%20Expansion%20and%20Re-ranking%20for%20Multimedia%20Retrieval.pdf)
- **Database-side feature augmentation**: reported to be beneficial to retrieval performance by enriching each *gallery* item's representation with its neighbors' features before search — again a batch, gallery-side operation requiring the full item set to be known ahead of time. — [diffusion/anchor-based retrieval synthesis](https://doi.org/10.3390/app15052535)
- A more directly relevant 2023 paper, **"If At First You Don't Succeed: Test Time Re-ranking for Zero-shot, Cross-domain Retrieval"**, targets exactly the zero-shot/cross-domain retrieval setting the project cares about, but its precise mechanism and online/offline requirements were not extracted in this research pass — flagged as a promising lead requiring follow-up reading. — [arXiv:2303.17703](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.17703)

### Inferences
- Given aero_eyes's actual setup (one fixed few-shot prototype fused from 3 reference photos, vs. a stream of one-off candidate crops with no persistent multi-item gallery), the *gallery-side* re-ranking techniques (k-reciprocal, diffusion, database augmentation) are not directly portable: there is no multi-item candidate structure to build a neighbor graph or Jaccard-distance matrix over. A meaningful adaptation would require artificially constructing a rolling "gallery" (e.g., a buffer of the last N high-confidence candidate crops from the current flight) and running k-reciprocal/diffusion re-ranking over *that*, which is a design choice the project would need to prototype and validate — it is not an established, literature-tested pattern.
- The one technique class that *is* naturally streaming-compatible without modification is **query-side (prototype-side) expansion**: since the project already fuses 3 reference photos into a prototype, and already has "online dynamic prototype update," this is conceptually the query-expansion idea already implemented in spirit — literature on AQE-style averaging of confirmed positive detections into the prototype could inform further refinement (e.g., weighted query expansion rather than simple averaging), but this should be cross-checked against the project's existing `online_dynamic_prototype_update.md` notes (not reviewed as part of this pass).

### Gaps
- No paper was found that specifically re-ranks in a true single-shot-streaming (one query, one-time-seen candidates, no persistent gallery) setting analogous to aero_eyes; all classical re-ranking literature assumes either a static gallery or an accumulating retrieval history. This is a genuine gap in the literature relative to the project's exact deployment scenario, not just a search limitation.
- The "Test Time Re-ranking for Zero-shot, Cross-domain Retrieval" paper (arXiv:2303.17703) was not fetched/read in full and may contain directly relevant technique(s) for the project's cross-domain, gallery-light setting — recommended as a priority follow-up read.

## Does a learned/calibrated temperature or scale on cosine similarity (like CLIP's logit_scale) meaningfully improve threshold-based separation?

### Takeaway
No — mathematically, dividing/multiplying cosine similarity by a single learned scalar (temperature/logit_scale) is a strictly monotonic transform, and monotonic transforms cannot change the rank-ordering of scores or the ROC/AUROC curve; therefore temperature/scale calibration alone **cannot** reduce the overlap between the project's TP (0.20–0.34) and FP (0.06–0.26) cosine distributions — it only stretches or compresses the number line, and any threshold chosen after temperature-scaling corresponds to an equivalent threshold before it. Genuinely improving separability requires either a non-monotonic/nonlinear transform of the raw score, or an entirely different statistic computed from additional information (background distribution, covariance structure, neighbor structure) — which is exactly what RMD and whitening provide and temperature scaling does not.

### Cited Findings
- "Temperature scaling is a monotonic rescaling that cannot change the order of pixels/samples by [score]; any decision rule that thresholds on that order is therefore invariant to temperature scaling by construction." — [general calibration/selective-prediction literature synthesis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.13262)
- "It is generally expected that calibration does not affect ranking metrics such as ROC-AUC... rank-preserving transforms cannot improve a discriminative metric," confirmed empirically: temperature scaling leaves uncertainty ranking unchanged while calibration-error metrics (e.g., ECE) may change. "Any monotonic scalar transformation applied to all test samples preserves score ranking and therefore cannot improve AUROC." — [Instance-Wise Monotonic Calibration by Constrained Transformation, arXiv:2507.06516](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06516); [selective prediction / calibration synthesis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07556)
- Important nuance: temperature scaling is a monotone rescaling of *logits*, but because it is applied *before* a non-linear function (softmax) in multi-class settings, it can induce a **non-monotone** re-ranking of final *probabilities* across different samples/classes — this subtlety is specific to softmax-normalized multi-class confidence comparisons, not to a single scalar similarity score thresholded directly (as in the project's binary match/no-match decision on one cosine or RMD value per crop). — [Instance-Wise Monotonic Calibration, arXiv:2507.06516](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06516)
- CLIP's own learned `logit_scale` parameter is applied identically to every embedding pair at inference (a single global multiplicative scalar) — it was learned to optimize the contrastive training loss's softmax temperature, not to maximize separability of any specific downstream TP/FP threshold decision, reinforcing that it is not designed as (and should not be treated as) a calibration fix for a downstream open-set threshold problem. — [Temperature scaling in CLIP synthesis](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/learnable-temperature-softmax)

### Inferences
- This directly answers the project's framing: yes, calibration for THIS purpose (improving a threshold-based TP/FP decision) requires a nonlinear/non-monotonic transform or an additional-information-bearing statistic — a single learned or hand-tuned temperature/scale on top of raw cosine similarity is provably incapable of doing this, no matter how well-tuned. This is consistent with, and provides theoretical justification for, why the project moved to RMD (which is not a monotonic function of cosine similarity — it incorporates covariance/background information cosine similarity does not have access to) rather than simply rescaling cosine.
- Any "adaptive/online threshold" mechanism the project already has is operating in the same monotonic-transform-limited regime *if* it only adjusts the threshold value itself on the same underlying raw-cosine score; the actual separability gain has to come from changing the underlying *scoring function* (e.g., RMD, whitened cosine, relative similarity), not from adjusting where the threshold sits on the same raw-score axis.

### Gaps
- No literature specific to vision embeddings (DINOv2/CLIP) doing a rigorous ablation of "temperature-scaled cosine similarity" vs. "RMD" vs. "whitened cosine" head-to-head on the same near-OOD/cross-domain benchmark was found; the monotonic-transform argument here is a general, model-agnostic mathematical fact (confirmed across the calibration literature) rather than a domain-specific empirical study, but it is a rigorous and directly applicable argument regardless of encoder.

## Beyond RMD, is there a documented alternative metric targeting the "narrow dynamic range" problem — relative similarity, L2 vs. cosine, triangle-inequality re-scoring?

### Takeaway
"Relative similarity" (target similarity minus similarity to the nearest negative/background) is a well-established idea across multiple literatures (RMD itself, triplet/margin losses, and speaker-verification cohort normalization) and is conceptually the right generalization direction beyond plain RMD. L2 distance on L2-normalized vectors is **mathematically monotonically equivalent to cosine similarity** and carries no additional ranking information — a common and confirmed point of confusion. A "triangle inequality for cosine similarity" exists in the literature but is designed for efficient similarity-search index pruning, not for improving score separability, so it is not itself a calibration fix.

### Cited Findings
- **L2 vs. cosine on normalized vectors**: "For normalized embeddings, cosine similarity and L2 distance become equivalent up to a monotonic transformation, and the choice between them has no effect on nearest-neighbor results." Formally, for unit vectors, ‖x − y‖² = 2 − 2·cos(x, y), so minimizing squared Euclidean distance is exactly equivalent to maximizing cosine similarity — "the two metrics induce the same neighbor ordering and are therefore interchangeable" once vectors are L2-normalized. — [L2 Distance vs Cosine Similarity synthesis](https://medium.com/@ishankgera.work/l2-distance-vs-cosine-similarity-the-hidden-connection-35c1ae121392); [Cosine Distance/Dot Product/Euclidean synthesis](https://medium.com/data-science-collective/cosine-distance-vs-dot-product-vs-euclidean-in-vector-similarity-search-227a6db32edb)
- This directly confirms the project's suspected point of confusion: switching from cosine similarity to L2 distance on the *same* L2-normalized DINOv2 embeddings will **not** change the ranking or separability of TP vs. FP at all — it is a purely monotonic re-expression of the identical information, so it cannot be a fix for the narrow-dynamic-range problem by itself (unlike RMD or whitening, which use *additional* information — a fitted background covariance — not present in raw cosine/L2).
- **Triangle inequality for cosine similarity**: proves bounds sim(x,y) ≥ sim(x,z)·sim(z,y) − √[(1−sim(x,z)²)(1−sim(z,y)²)] and the symmetric upper bound, enabling classical metric-indexing structures (VP-trees, M-trees, Cover-trees) to prune candidates for cosine similarity search. This is an **indexing/efficiency** tool for pruning candidates in large-scale search, not a re-scoring technique that changes or improves the separability of TP vs. FP similarity distributions. — [Aumüller & Ceccarello, "A Triangle Inequality for Cosine Similarity," arXiv:2107.04071](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.04071)
- **Relative-similarity-style scoring is a recurring, cross-domain pattern**: (a) RMD itself is defined as target-Mahalanobis minus background-Mahalanobis (a relative/differential score); (b) speaker-verification Z-norm/T-norm/AS-Norm normalize a raw cosine score using the mean/std of similarity against a background cohort — effectively (score − cohort_mean) / cohort_std, a relative/standardized score; (c) few-shot open-set recognition work explicitly frames matching as "distance to positive prototype" compared against a threshold, with papers noting that "a few-shot classifier may have similar detection scores for negative and positive queries, requiring different thresholds... a threshold that works for one task may fail in others" — precisely the narrow-separation problem the project describes, and part of the motivation for relative/margin-based scoring over absolute thresholds in this literature. — [DKU-MSXF AS-Norm, arXiv:2308.08766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.08766); [Few-shot open-set recognition threshold-tuning synthesis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031320324001511)
- **Margin/triplet-based metric learning** (anchor, positive, negative triplets) is the training-time analogue of relative similarity scoring — optimizing embeddings so that sim(anchor, positive) − sim(anchor, negative) is large by construction — but this requires access to negatives *during training/fine-tuning*, which is a different intervention than a frozen-encoder, inference-time re-scoring fix. — [few-shot open-set/triplet loss synthesis](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2023/0390.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because L2-vs-cosine is a dead end (monotonically equivalent, confirmed above), and temperature/scale calibration is also a dead end (monotonic, per the previous section), the credible remaining directions beyond RMD that specifically target the narrow-dynamic-range problem are: (1) whitening/z-scoring against a background distribution (same family as RMD, see Q2), and (2) explicit "relative similarity" = sim(query, prototype) − sim(query, nearest-negative-or-background-cluster), which is a simpler, non-Gaussian-assumption cousin of RMD that could be computed online per-frame against a running buffer of recent negative/confuser embeddings without needing to fit a full covariance matrix — this may be a useful lightweight complement or sanity-check alongside RMD, especially if the background covariance in RMD is hard to estimate reliably from limited background samples in a streaming setting.
- The AS-Norm speaker-verification precedent is a strong independent signal that background-relative normalization (of which RMD is a Gaussian-model-based instance) is the single most literature-validated general answer to "narrow, overlapping raw-similarity range" problems across multiple unrelated fields (NLP anisotropy correction, OOD detection, and biometric verification) — this triangulates well with the project's own choice to move to RMD, and suggests further gains are more likely from refining the *background modeling* (e.g., adaptive/dynamic covariance, non-Gaussian background models, k-NN-based local background estimation as an alternative to a single global Gaussian) than from alternative *distance metrics* per se.

### Gaps
- No direct literature was found proposing or evaluating a non-Gaussian "relative similarity" (nearest-negative-margin) score specifically as an alternative/complement to Mahalanobis-based OOD scoring for embedding retrieval; this appears to be an underexplored or at least under-published combination, inferred by analogy from triplet-loss training objectives and open-set threshold literature rather than found as a direct citation.
- Whether a k-NN-based local background estimate (vs. RMD's single global Gaussian) would help specifically in the project's cross-domain drone setting was not addressed in any source found; this is a reasonable research question the project could test empirically but is not literature-backed here.
