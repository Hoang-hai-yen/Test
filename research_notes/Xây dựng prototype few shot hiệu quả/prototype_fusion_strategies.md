# Prototype Construction / Fusion Techniques for Few-Shot Visual Matching

## What does Prototypical Networks (Snell et al., NeurIPS 2017) and follow-ups say about prototype quality vs. number of shots and fusion method? Is mean-pooling near-optimal for small K (e.g. K=3)?

### Takeaway
Snell et al.'s original formulation defines the prototype simply as the arithmetic mean of embedded support examples, justified as the Bregman-divergence-optimal point estimate under the assumed embedding-space clustering structure; later theoretical work (NeurIPS 2022) shows mean-pooling's shortfall relative to more careful estimators is a function of within-class/between-class variance and shrinks as K grows — implying the *gap* between mean-pooling and a better estimator is likely largest, not smallest, at very small K such as K=3.

### Cited Findings
- The prototype for class c is defined as p_c = (1/|S_c|) Σ f_θ(x_i) — the mean of embedded support points — and Snell et al. justify this via a connection to mixture density estimation / Bregman divergences, under which the mean is the provably optimal single-point summary if the embedding is well-clustered. — [Prototypical Networks for Few-shot Learning (NeurIPS 2017)](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~zemel/documents/prototypical_networks_nips_2017.pdf) / [NeurIPS proceedings page](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/6996-prototypical-networks-for-few-shot-learning)
- "A Closer Look at Prototype Classifier for Few-shot Image Classification" (NeurIPS 2022) derives a generalization bound for the mean-pooled prototype classifier showing that performance depends critically on (a) the variance of feature-vector norms and (b) the ratio of within-class to between-class variance; the paper states this term "depends on K (number of shots)" and explicitly that it "diminishes as K increases," so "the performance gain of the feature-transformation methods decreases" as K grows. — [A Closer Look at Prototype Classifier for Few-shot Image Classification, arXiv:2110.05076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05076)
- That same paper shows that L2-normalizing features and minimizing the within-class/between-class variance ratio lets a plain (still mean-pooled) prototype classifier match meta-learned classifiers without retraining — i.e., the fix is to normalize/transform the *embeddings* before mean-pooling, not to replace mean-pooling with a different aggregation rule. — [arXiv:2110.05076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05076)
- "Alleviating the Sample Selection Bias in Few-shot Learning by Removing Projection to the Centroid" (TCPR, NeurIPS 2022) identifies "sample selection bias": with very few support samples, those samples tend to cluster near the task centroid (mean of all class centroids in the task), which biases the resulting per-class mean prototype. Their fix, Task Centroid Projection Removing (TCPR), removes the feature dimension aligned with the (estimated) task centroid direction before/after pooling, and "reliably improve[s] classification accuracy across various feature extractors, training algorithms and datasets." — [TCPR, arXiv:2210.16834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.16834); [code](https://github.com/KikimorMay/FSL-TCPR)
- TCPR estimates the task centroid using auxiliary base-class features "similar to" each support feature, because "the exact task centroid cannot be accurately obtained from limited data" — an implicit acknowledgment that mean-pooling degrades specifically because K is small. — [arXiv:2210.16834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.16834)
- "Free Lunch for Few-shot Learning: Distribution Calibration" (Yang, Liu & Xu, ICLR 2021, Oral) does not modify the fusion rule for the K shots themselves, but instead calibrates the estimated per-class *distribution* (mean and covariance) by borrowing statistics from semantically similar base classes that have abundant data, then samples synthetic features from the calibrated distribution to augment the classifier — a workaround for the fact that K samples give an unreliable mean/covariance estimate. It reports ~5% accuracy improvement on miniImageNet over the next-best comparable method, using only a pretrained feature extractor and no extra learned parameters. — [Free Lunch for Few-shot Learning, arXiv:2101.06395](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.06395)

### Inferences
- None of the "mean is optimal" theoretical justifications in the original ProtoNet paper are K-specific; they rely on population-level clustering assumptions that are least accurate exactly when K is smallest. Combined with the NeurIPS 2022 finding that bias/variance-related error terms shrink with K, the literature implies mean-pooling is *further* from optimal at K=3 than at K=5/K=20, not closer to optimal — directly answering the project's question in the negative (mean-pooling is not "near-optimal" at K=3; if anything K=3 is where correction techniques should matter most).
- The two NeurIPS 2022 papers converge on a common pattern for query-agnostic, training-free fixes: don't replace the mean with a different aggregation of the K shots, but (a) normalize/whiten features first (L2-norm + variance-ratio minimization) and/or (b) remove a specific bias direction (task-centroid projection) from the pooled feature — both are cheap, inference-time-only operations applicable directly to a 3-image reference set.

### Gaps
- No paper found gives an isolated, controlled ablation of "mean vs. max vs. median vs. weighted-mean of exactly K=3 shots" holding all else fixed; most K-dependence results are reported at the standard FSL benchmark values (K=1, K=5), with K=1 and K=5 as the two anchor points and no K=3 curve point specifically published in the sources found.

## Prototype rectification / calibration methods (Liu et al. ECCV 2020 and follow-ups): what do they do differently from plain averaging, and are they query-agnostic?

### Takeaway
BD-CSPN (Liu et al., ECCV 2020) is explicitly a two-part method: the intra-class "bias diminishing" step reweights the K support examples by their cosine similarity to the initial mean prototype (a softmax-style self-weighting that in principle needs *only* the support/reference set, i.e., is query-agnostic if the query-augmentation term is dropped), while the cross-class "bias diminishing" (feature-shifting) step explicitly requires query-set statistics and is therefore not usable at prototype-build time with only the K references.

### Cited Findings
- The paper (Liu, Song & Qin, "Prototype Rectification for Few-Shot Learning," arXiv:1911.10713 / ECCV 2020) frames the problem as two biases: **intra-class bias** (support-set samples for one class deviating from the true class mean due to the narrow sampling distribution) and **cross-class bias** (support and query feature distributions being shifted relative to each other because the model was trained on different base classes). — [arXiv:1911.10713](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10713)
- Intra-class bias diminishing, Eq. 5–6: the rectified prototype is P'_n = Σ_{i=1}^{K+Z} w_{i,n} · X̄'_{i,n}, with weight w_{i,n} = exp(ε·cos(X'_{i,n}, P_n)) / Σ_j exp(ε·cos(X'_{j,n}, P_n)) — i.e., a softmax over cosine similarity of each (normalized) sample to the current mean prototype, applied to the K original support samples **plus** Z pseudo-labeled query samples. — [arXiv:1911.10713 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/1911.10713)
- Cross-class bias diminishing, Eq. 8: a "feature shifting" term ξ = (1/|S|)Σ X̄_{i,s} − (1/|Q|)Σ X̄_{j,q}, i.e. the mean difference between the (normalized) support-set feature distribution and the query-set feature distribution — this term is added to prototypes and **requires access to the query set's feature statistics**. The paper states this operates "in transductive setting." — [arXiv:1911.10713 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/1911.10713)
- Reported results (Table 4 in the paper) on miniImageNet: baseline mean-prototype ("CSPN") gets 61.84% (1-shot) / 78.64% (5-shot); the full rectified method ("BD-CSPN," using both intra- and cross-class correction) gets 70.31% (1-shot, +8.47pp) / 81.89% (5-shot, +3.25pp). On tieredImageNet: 69.20%→78.74% (1-shot, +9.54pp) and 84.31%→86.92% (5-shot, +2.61pp). — [arXiv:1911.10713](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10713); [ECCV proceedings PDF](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/papers/123460715.pdf)
- Note the gain is consistently **larger at 1-shot than at 5-shot** in every reported pair (+8.47 vs +3.25 on miniImageNet; +9.54 vs +2.61 on tieredImageNet) — direct evidence that rectification helps more when K is smaller, consistent with the K-dependence pattern found in the bias/variance papers above.

### Inferences
- The intra-class weighting formula (Eq. 5–6) is mathematically self-contained if the Z (pseudo-labeled query) term is set to zero — it becomes a pure self-referential softmax reweighting of the K support/reference embeddings by their similarity to their own mean, requiring no query or target-domain data. This is a directly transplantable, training-free, query-agnostic technique for the project: compute the plain mean, then re-weight the 3 reference embeddings by softmax(ε·cos(embedding, mean)) and recompute a weighted mean — an "iterate-to-convergence" self-attention-like refinement that automatically down-weights an outlier reference (one dissimilar to the other two).
- The cross-class bias-diminishing half of BD-CSPN, and the paper's transductive framing overall, is the piece that is *not* usable without target-domain (query/candidate-crop) data — this maps directly onto the "domain_calibration" bucket the project has already implemented, not the "build-time, references-only" bucket.
- The pattern that gains shrink from 1-shot to 5-shot suggests, by extrapolation, that K=3 should see a correction benefit intermediate between the reported 1-shot and 5-shot deltas — i.e., a meaningful but not huge improvement (order of a few percentage points) from adding self-weighted reweighting alone (without the transductive cross-class term).

### Gaps
- No paper found isolates the intra-class-only (query-agnostic) component's accuracy delta separately from the full BD-CSPN (intra + cross-class); the reported +8.47pp / +3.25pp figures are for the combined transductive method, so the query-agnostic-only contribution size is not directly measured in the sources found — treat the self-weighting idea as principled but not independently benchmarked.
- No source found gives a K=3-specific number for BD-CSPN or successor "prototype calibration" papers (e.g. Prototype Completion for Few-Shot Learning, arXiv:2108.05010, found in search but not fetched/verified in depth) — flagged but not verified with a primary-source read.

## Attention-based / learned-weighting fusion of multiple shots (Dynamic Few-Shot Visual Learning, TADAM, FEAT): do they beat mean-pooling, by how much, and do they need training?

### Takeaway
All three landmark papers require meta-training a learned module (they are not usable training-free), and — critically — most of them do **not** actually change how the K shots of a single class are fused (that stays mean-pooling); instead they adapt the *metric/feature space* (TADAM) or adapt prototypes *across classes* in a task using inter-class context (FEAT), while only Gidaris & Komodakis's attention-based weight generator is a genuine alternative to mean-pooling for combining multiple support exemplars into one class weight vector, and even that is trained end-to-end on a large base-class dataset.

### Cited Findings
- **TADAM** (Oreshkin, Rodriguez & Lacoste, NeurIPS 2018) proposes "metric scaling" (a learned temperature on distances) and "metric task conditioning" via a Task Embedding Network (TEN) that generates FiLM-style conditioning parameters for the feature extractor based on the task's mean class prototype; metric scaling alone gives up to 14% accuracy improvement on miniImageNet 5-way 5-shot for some metrics. Based on the paper's own framing ("task-dependent metric space" / "metric task conditioning"), the mechanism conditions the *feature extractor / distance metric* on the task, not the aggregation rule used to combine the K shots into a prototype — i.e., TADAM still mean-pools shots, it just changes what gets pooled. — [TADAM, arXiv:1805.10123](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10123)
- **FEAT** (Ye et al., CVPR 2020) trains a set-to-set function T(·) — best implemented as a Transformer with self-attention — that maps the *set of already-mean-pooled class prototypes* P=(p_1,...,p_N) for an N-way episode to a set of task-adapted prototypes P'=(p'_1,...,p'_N). The transformation operates across the N per-class prototypes in a task (making each class prototype more discriminative relative to the other classes present), not across the K within-class shots that produced each p_i in the first place. FEAT was benchmarked against BiLSTM, DeepSets, and GCN as alternative set-to-set functions, and the Transformer variant performed best of those four. — [FEAT, arXiv:1812.03664](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03664); [code](https://github.com/Sha-Lab/FEAT)
- Reported numbers: on miniImageNet with a ResNet backbone, 1-shot 5-way accuracy is FEAT 66.78% vs. ProtoNet 62.39% — a +4.39pp gain — though this experimental setup measures FEAT's cross-class task-adaptation benefit (at K=1, so there is no intra-class fusion happening at all in either method), not an intra-class multi-shot fusion improvement. — [search-aggregated result citing the FEAT paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03664)
- **Dynamic Few-Shot Visual Learning without Forgetting** (Gidaris & Komodakis, CVPR 2018) explicitly proposes an "attention-based few-shot classification weight generator" that composes a novel class weight vector by attending over a memory of base-class weight vectors (cosine-similarity attention kernel + softmax, using learned query/key transforms), as an alternative to (and compared against) a simpler averaging-based weight generator baseline. The attention-based generator is meta-trained jointly with a cosine-similarity classifier head. — [arXiv:1804.09458](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09458)
- All three methods (TADAM's TEN, FEAT's set-to-set Transformer, Gidaris & Komodakis's attention weight generator) require an offline meta-training phase over many base classes with many episodes; none are presented or evaluated as training-free, inference-time-only techniques usable directly on a novel-object encoder like DINOv2/CLIP with zero fine-tuning. — [arXiv:1805.10123](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10123); [arXiv:1812.03664](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03664); [arXiv:1804.09458](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09458)
- In an adjacent domain (open-vocabulary object detection), Kaul et al. ("Multi-Modal Classifiers for Open-Vocabulary Object Detection," ICML 2023) explicitly build a learned "visual aggregator" (attention/set-function based) that fuses multiple image exemplars into one vision-based class classifier, ablated against a simple mean of exemplar embeddings as baseline — but this aggregator is also meta-trained, not training-free. — [arXiv:2306.05493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)
- OVMR ("Open-Vocabulary Recognition with Multi-Modal References," arXiv:2406.04675) instead proposes a genuinely **parameter-free, training-free** "preference-based fusion module": at inference, it evaluates each of several candidate classifiers (text-based, vision-based, multi-modal) directly on the small set of exemplar images themselves, computes an F1-style score per classifier, and combines them via softmax-normalized preference weights — no learned parameters, usable on unseen classes with just the provided exemplars. Reported ablation: simple mean fusion reaches 79.94%–81.45% average accuracy depending on variant, while the F1-based preference fusion reaches 82.34% — a roughly 1–2.4pp gain over naive mean fusion. — [OVMR, arXiv:2406.04675](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04675v1)

### Inferences
- The headline "attention beats mean-pooling" claims in the few-shot literature are almost all about fusing information *across classes in a task* or *across base-class prior knowledge*, not about fusing multiple same-class shots — this is an important distinction the project should not conflate. For the project's use case (fusing 3 same-object reference crops with no other classes/base-class memory available at inference), the directly relevant precedent is much thinner than the "attention-based few-shot learning" literature suggests at first glance.
- OVMR's "preference-based fusion" is the most directly transplantable idea for the project: instead of (or in addition to) a segmentation-confidence weight, weight each of the 3 reference embeddings' contribution to the final prototype by how well that single embedding, used alone, would "recognize" the *other two* references (a self-consistency/leave-one-out score) — this requires no training and only the 3 reference images themselves.
- Given that none of the strongest "beats mean-pooling" results in this section come from training-free methods evaluated on genuine within-class shot fusion, the project should treat "attention/learned fusion beats mean" as **not empirically established for its exact regime** (training-free, K=3, same-class shot fusion) — the honest answer to "do these consistently beat mean-pooling" is: for cross-class/task adaptation, yes and by several points; for training-free same-class shot fusion specifically, the evidence is thin to absent in the papers reviewed.

### Gaps
- No paper found runs a controlled ablation of "mean-pool K shots" vs. "attention-pool K shots" *for the same class*, holding the downstream metric/classifier fixed, at K=1/3/5/10/20 — the standard few-shot literature conflates within-class shot aggregation with across-class task adaptation, and this specific ablation (which the project's key question asks for) does not appear to exist as a clean, isolated experiment in the sources found.
- Exact Kaul et al. (ICML 2023) ablation numbers (visual-aggregator vs. mean, by number of exemplars) could not be extracted — the PDF fetch failed to yield readable text and a follow-up search did not surface the specific table; this remains unverified and should be treated as a gap rather than assumed to favor either side.

## Outlier/quality-aware fusion: automatically down-weighting a "bad" reference without ground truth

### Takeaway
Multiple lines of work — robust-statistics mean estimation (median-of-means), probabilistic/uncertainty-weighted face-template fusion, and few-shot "noisy support" papers — all support the idea of automatically down-weighting an outlier reference using only internal agreement/consistency signals (no ground truth needed), but each has caveats for a K=3 regime: median-of-means needs enough samples to form multiple blocks (marginal at K=3), and uncertainty-weighted fusion (PFE-style) needs training a small uncertainty-estimation head, so it is not literally training-free unless such a head is reused from an existing model.

### Cited Findings
- **Median-of-Means (MoM) mean embedding estimation**: MONK ("Outlier-Robust Mean Embedding Estimation by Median-of-Means," Lerasle et al.) shows that the classical empirical mean of a kernel/feature embedding "can be arbitrarily severely affected even by a single outlier," and that partitioning samples into blocks and taking the median of per-block means yields an estimator with "optimal sub-Gaussian deviation bounds" that is robust to outliers, without needing any labels — purely a function of the raw sample embeddings. — [MONK, arXiv:1802.04784](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04784)
- Related "median-of-means classifiers" (replacing mean losses with blockwise medians in empirical risk minimization) are "provably robust to outliers" while matching standard ERM performance under weak assumptions — a general robust-statistics justification for preferring median-like aggregation over naive mean when contamination is possible. — [search-aggregated summary of MoM literature, arXiv:1802.04784](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04784)
- **Self-weighting via similarity to the current mean** (BD-CSPN's intra-class term, see above) is itself a form of automatic, ground-truth-free outlier down-weighting: an outlier reference embedding, being less cosine-similar to the initial mean, receives a smaller softmax weight w_i in the next-iteration prototype — this needs no query data and no labels, only the K references. — [Liu et al., arXiv:1911.10713](https://arxiv.org/html/1911.10713)
- A domain-specific application (few-shot classification of bearing surface defects) implements exactly this idea with a **Sum-of-Absolute-Differences (SAD)-based weighting in embedding space to down-weight outlier support samples and form robust class prototypes**, reported as improving robustness over unweighted mean prototypes in that application. — [Class prototype rectification and multi-scale feature measurement for few-shot classification of bearing surface defects, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13447918/)
- **RapNets** ("Robust Few-Shot Learning for User-Provided Data," Lu et al., IEEE TNNLS) address realistic support sets that may contain "representation outliers" and "label outliers" from noisy user-provided data, using a BiLSTM-based attentive module that "dynamically adjust[s] the weights of training samples to mitigate the impact of unreliable data" — but this attentive module must be (meta-)trained, so it is not training-free at inference on a novel object with no such training history. — [RapNets, IEEE TNNLS, doi:10.1109/TNNLS.2020.2984710](https://doi.org/10.1109/tnnls.2020.2984710)
- **Probabilistic Face Embeddings (PFE)** (Shi & Jain, ICCV 2019) represent each face image as a Gaussian (mean + per-dimension variance) rather than a point, where the variance serves as an inverse quality/confidence signal; multiple images of one identity are then fused via variance-weighted (precision-weighted) combination of their Gaussian means — mathematically a quality-weighted mean where higher-uncertainty (lower-quality) images contribute less. A follow-up ("Fast and Reliable Probabilistic Face Embeddings...") proposes a **feature fusion method based on temperature scaling that adjusts fusion weights by image quality** for multi-image/video templates. — [PFE, ICCV 2019](http://biometrics.cse.msu.edu/Publications/Face/ShiJain_ProbabilisticFaceEmbeddings_arXiv2019.pdf); [Fast and reliable PFE, arXiv:2102.04075](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.04075)
- Caveat: PFE-style uncertainty weighting requires training an uncertainty-estimation branch/head (or a full uncertainty-aware embedding network) — it is not obtainable purely from an off-the-shelf DINOv2/CLIP/SigLIP encoder's output without adding and training such a head, so it should be classified as "requires some training investment," not purely training-free, even though it needs no target-domain (query) data. — [PFE, ICCV 2019](http://biometrics.cse.msu.edu/Publications/Face/ShiJain_ProbabilisticFaceEmbeddings_arXiv2019.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a training-free, query-agnostic, K=3 setting, the most practically applicable outlier-down-weighting technique from this literature is the **self-referential agreement weighting** pattern shared by BD-CSPN's intra-class term, the SAD-weighted bearing-defect paper, and (in spirit) OVMR's preference fusion: score each reference embedding by its similarity/agreement with the *other* references (leave-one-out consistency, or similarity to the current running mean), convert to a softmax or normalized weight, and use that as the fusion weight — this can be layered on top of (or combined multiplicatively with) the project's existing segmentation-confidence weighting.
- Median-of-means is a theoretically strong idea but is a poor operational fit at K=3, because MoM's robustness comes from partitioning many samples into several blocks and taking a median across block-means; with only 3 samples there are not enough points to form multiple meaningful blocks (in the degenerate limit, block size 1 reduces to taking the literal per-dimension/per-sample median of 3 points, which is a valid but very blunt robust estimator — better thought of as "coordinate-wise median of 3 embeddings" or "geometric median of 3 points" rather than true block-based MoM).

### Gaps
- No source found reports a quantitative accuracy comparison of "similarity/agreement-weighted mean" vs. plain mean specifically for K=3 (all robust-outlier papers found evaluate at K=5 or larger, or in non-few-shot fusion settings like face templates with many enrollment images); the direction of the effect (down-weighting helps) is well supported, but the magnitude for K=3 specifically is not documented in a source found.
- Could not verify a specific "agreement-based weighting" paper by name beyond the ones cited (BD-CSPN, the bearing-defect SAD paper, and PFE/RapNets); a broader/dedicated survey of "automatic outlier rejection among K reference images with no ground truth" specific to visual matching was not located.

## Concatenation + PCA fusion: literature precedent and when it outperforms mean-pooling

### Takeaway
Concatenate-then-reduce (via PCA or similar) has clear precedent in multi-view/multi-scale image retrieval and template-based face recognition (e.g., multi-scale CNN feature concatenation followed by PCA compression, and "Pooling Faces" style template pooling), and the literature's consistent finding is that concatenation+PCA helps when the individual views/shots carry **complementary, non-redundant information** (different scales, different modalities, different poses/viewpoints) — but for K homogeneous close-up shots of the same object from similar viewpoints (as in this project), the complementary-information assumption is weaker, and PCA on only 3 points is also statistically underdetermined.

### Cited Findings
- In multi-scale/multi-view CNN feature fusion for remote-sensing image retrieval, a standard pipeline resizes an image to different scales, extracts a CNN feature per scale, **concatenates** the per-scale feature vectors, and then **compresses the concatenated vector via PCA** to a unified lower dimension before retrieval/matching. — [Exploiting Deep Features for Remote Sensing Image Retrieval, arXiv:1707.07321](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.07321)
- In multi-view embedding for web images, researchers reduce each individual view's features to ~500 PCA dimensions and then concatenate the reduced views together; PCA-then-concatenate on the *individual* feature types costs "less than 1% decrease" in image-to-image retrieval performance versus using the raw features, but the resulting **combined (concatenated) features significantly outperform each individual feature type used alone.** — [A Multi-View Embedding Space for Modeling Internet Images, Tags, and their Semantics, arXiv:1212.4522](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1212.4522)
- General fusion-strategy taxonomy for image retrieval explicitly lists "feature concatenation, weighted score fusion, and dimensionality-reduction on the concatenated space (PCA, LDA)" as standard, competing options — concat+PCA is a named, well-established member of this family, not a novel technique. — [search-aggregated summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.05075)
- In face recognition, "Pooling Faces: Template based Face Recognition with Pooled Face Images" (CVPR 2016 workshop) demonstrates that **simple pooling of multiple face images of a subject** (grouped and averaged by pose/yaw, i.e., not naive global mean but pose-stratified pooling) is a strong, simple template-construction baseline. — [Pooling Faces, CVPR 2016 Workshop](https://www.cv-foundation.org/openaccess/content_cvpr_2016_workshops/w4/papers/Hassner_Pooling_Faces_Template_CVPR_2016_paper.pdf)
- Template Adaptation (Crosswhite et al., "Template Adaptation for Face Verification and Identification," 2016/2017) — a widely cited IJB-A benchmark paper — constructs the base template feature simply as the **unit-normalized mean of media (image) features** in a template (i.e., plain mean-pooling, not concat+PCA), and then trains a **template-specific linear SVM** (one-vs-rest, using the template's media as positives and a large external negative set) on top of deep CNN features. The paper reports that this combination "outperforms the state-of-the-art by a wide margin" on IJB-A, and that other refinements (metric learning, 2D/3D alignment) converge to nearly the same top performance once combined with template adaptation — suggesting the SVM-adaptation step, not the pooling rule, is what does the heavy lifting, and plain mean-pooling of the raw per-image features remains the template feature construction step. — [Template Adaptation for Face Verification and Identification, arXiv:1603.03958](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.03958) / [full paper PDF](https://www.robots.ox.ac.uk/~vgg/publications/2017/Crosswhite17/crosswhite17.pdf)
- A recent paper mixing image and text prototypes in CLIP-based few-shot classification ("Cross-Modal Prototype Alignment and Mixing for Training-Free Few-Shot Classification," 2026) frames prototype mixing explicitly as a **bias-variance trade-off / shrinkage estimator** problem: naively mixing an image-derived prototype with a text-derived one adds noise from instance-specific background/context, so they instead project the image prototype onto the principal directions of the semantic text-embedding subspace before mixing, which "further improve[s] classification." This is not the same problem as multi-shot fusion (it fuses image + text modalities, not K reference images), but the underlying bias-variance/shrinkage framing is directly relevant conceptually: naive concatenation of correlated, low-diversity views inflates variance without adding proportional signal, and dimensionality reduction is a way to recover a shrinkage-like effect. — [arXiv:2603.24528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24528)

### Inferences
- The literature precedent for concat+PCA is strongest when the "views" being concatenated are genuinely complementary (different image scales, different modalities, different feature extractors, or pose-stratified subsets), and comes with an explicit caveat even in the best case (the ~1% retrieval-quality cost of PCA on individual features, before concatenation gain outweighs it). For 3 close-up photos of the *same object*, likely from similar viewpoints/lighting captured in one shoot, the "complementary information" assumption underlying concat+PCA's success elsewhere is weaker — the 3 embeddings are probably highly correlated, so concatenation mostly repeats redundant signal, and PCA fit on only 3 data points is rank-deficient/statistically unstable (PCA on N=3 samples in a high-dimensional embedding space can extract at most 2 non-trivial components, and those components' directions are unreliable estimates of true intra-class variance).
- Two independent lines of evidence (Crosswhite's face template baseline, and Snell et al.'s original justification) converge on: when multiple images are genuinely instances of "the same underlying identity/object" (not complementary views), the literature's default and most battle-tested choice is still mean-pooling the raw features, with any further gains coming from *reweighting* (robust/attention-based) or *downstream adaptation* (SVM, feature-space calibration) rather than from concatenation.
- Given this, the project's own "concat_then_pca" option has real literature precedent as a *technique family*, but the specific conditions favoring it (complementary/diverse views, moderately large N for stable PCA) are not well matched by "K=3 close-up photos of one object" — the literature would predict concat+PCA is more likely to help if the 3 references were deliberately diverse (e.g., different angles/lighting/occlusion states) than if they are visually similar close-ups, and less likely to help, and more likely to be unstable, than plain (possibly reweighted) mean-pooling at N=3.

### Gaps
- No source found directly benchmarks concat+PCA against mean-pooling specifically for fusing K=3 same-object/same-identity image embeddings (all concat+PCA precedent found is either multi-scale/multi-view of a single image, or multi-modal fusion, not multi-shot-of-one-object fusion) — this is an extrapolation from adjacent literature, not a directly measured result, and should be flagged as such to the project.
- No numeric comparison found of PCA component count vs. sample count stability specifically in the N=3 regime; the "PCA is unstable with few samples" claim is a general statistical inference, not a cited empirical result from the sources gathered.

## Findings specific to very small K (K=3) vs. commonly studied K=5/K=10/K=20 — does fusion strategy matter more or less at K=3?

### Takeaway
Every K-dependent result found in this research points the same direction: the benefit of moving beyond plain mean-pooling (via rectification, bias-removal, or distribution calibration) is largest at the smallest K studied (K=1) and shrinks monotonically as K grows toward 5/10/20; no source studies K=3 in isolation, but interpolating between the reported K=1 and K=5 deltas, and the explicit "diminishes as K increases" theoretical statement, both support that fusion-strategy choice matters *more*, not less, at K=3 than at larger K.

### Cited Findings
- BD-CSPN's reported gains over plain mean-pooling shrink from 1-shot to 5-shot in both benchmarks tested: miniImageNet +8.47pp (1-shot) → +3.25pp (5-shot); tieredImageNet +9.54pp (1-shot) → +2.61pp (5-shot). — [Liu et al., arXiv:1911.10713](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10713)
- "A Closer Look at Prototype Classifier for Few-shot Image Classification" states directly that the error term capturing (variance of feature-norms) × (within/between-class variance ratio) "depends on K" and "diminishes as K increases," causing "the performance gain of the feature-transformation methods" to decrease with larger K. — [arXiv:2110.05076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05076)
- TCPR's sample-selection-bias problem is explicitly framed as arising "when the positions of support samples are in the vicinity of the task centroid" under limited data — a problem whose severity is inherently a decreasing function of K (more samples make the empirical mean less susceptible to a few samples landing near the centroid by chance). — [arXiv:2210.16834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.16834)
- The standard few-shot benchmarks (miniImageNet, tieredImageNet) almost universally report only K=1 and K=5 as the two shot settings; broader few-shot-object-detection benchmarks sometimes extend to K=10/K=20/K=30, but none of the sources retrieved in this research explicitly isolate K=3 as a studied point. — [general pattern observed across all benchmark papers cited above, e.g. arXiv:1911.10713](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10713)

### Inferences
- Because K=3 sits between the two most commonly reported anchor points (K=1 and K=5), and every source shows the correction-method advantage strictly decreasing from K=1 to K=5, the most defensible inference is that a rectification/reweighting method's edge over plain mean at K=3 should be intermediate but closer to the (larger) K=1 gain than to the K=5 gain, since bias/variance-driven error typically decreases roughly as 1/K or 1/√K, which is a convex (rapidly-decreasing) function — meaning most of the "excess error" that correction methods can remove is already present by K=3, not spread evenly down to K=5.
- This supports the project's intuition that fusion strategy is *worth investing in* at K=3, more so than it would be at a hypothetical K=10 or K=20 regime where mean-pooling's shortcomings are shown to largely wash out.

### Gaps
- This is the weakest-evidenced section: no primary source in this research explicitly reports a K=3 data point for any fusion/rectification method against a mean-pooling baseline. The claim that "K=3 matters more than K=5+" is an interpolation from monotonic trends reported at K=1 and K=5, not a directly measured result, and should be presented to the project as a well-supported inference rather than a cited fact.
- No source was found studying the specific question of whether, below some small K, a rectification method could become *unstable or harmful* (e.g., because the self-weighting softmax in BD-CSPN, with only 3 points, could overfit to noise and aggressively down-weight a genuinely good reference by chance) — this is a plausible risk at very small K that the project should test empirically rather than assume away.
