# Online/Streaming/Dynamic Prototype (Template) Update Strategies — SOT Tracking & Few-Shot/Open-Set Recognition Literature

## Key Question 1: In SOT literature, what are established strategies for WHEN and HOW MUCH to update a tracking template from newly confirmed detections, and what safeguards exist against drift?

### Takeaway
The dominant, training-free pattern in modern SOT is: (a) gate every candidate update with a learned or heuristic confidence/reliability signal before it is allowed anywhere near the template, and (b) even when gated, prefer a *bounded, slow* fusion with the template (EMA-style or interval-batched selection) rather than hard replacement — because full reliance on the original template causes drift over time, but full reliance on the newest frame causes catastrophic contamination from a single bad detection.

### Cited Findings
- Siamese trackers (SiamFC-family) classically do **not** update the initial template at all "due to background noise and tracking speed," but "complete reliance on the initial template will cause catastrophic drift and tracking failure over time" in long sequences — i.e., the field explicitly frames no-update vs. naive-update as two failure modes bracketing the correct middle ground. — [PMC: Robust Template Adjustment Siamese Network](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7923413/)
- "Too frequent template updates per frame may introduce too much background information into the target model, resulting in template degradation and increasing the probability of tracking drift. To avoid corrupting templates, it is important to manage when and how the templates are updated." — [PMC: Robust Template Adjustment Siamese Network](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7923413/)
- STARK (Learning Spatio-Temporal Transformer for Visual Tracking) adds a **dedicated score head** whose only job is to judge the validity of a candidate dynamic template *before* it is allowed to replace the previous one; "improper uses of temporal information may hurt performance and it is important to filter out unreliable templates." Removing the score head causes a significant performance drop in their ablation, i.e., empirical evidence that ungated dynamic templates hurt more than they help. — [Learning Spatio-Temporal Transformer for Visual Tracking (STARK)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.17154); confirmed via secondary source — [EURASIP: Long-term tracking with transformer and template update](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13634-022-00954-4)
- STARK's concrete operating parameters (as reported by a downstream reproduction/analysis): confidence-score threshold τ = 0.50, and the dynamic template is only even considered for update **every 200 frames** — i.e., gating is combined with a fixed refresh interval, not per-frame updating. If the score is below τ at that checkpoint, the previous dynamic template is kept instead of being replaced. — [Improving Accuracy and Generalization for Efficient Visual Tracking (Pith Review analysis)](https://pith.science/paper/2411.18855)
- MixFormer's Score Prediction Module (SPM) follows the same "gate + batch interval" pattern: it computes a confidence score every frame, stores (candidate, score) pairs, and only at the end of a fixed update interval (default 200) does it pick the single **highest-scoring** candidate from that window as the new online template — deliberately avoiding a per-frame hard swap. — [MixFormer: End-to-End Tracking With Iterative Mixed Attention (CVPR 2022)](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Cui_MixFormer_End-to-End_Tracking_With_Iterative_Mixed_Attention_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf)
- UpdateNet (ICCV 2019) frames the problem explicitly against the pre-existing standard: "this template is linearly combined with the accumulated template from the previous frame, resulting in an exponential decay of information over time" — confirming that **linear/EMA fusion was already the established baseline** in Siamese trackers (e.g., SiamFC, DaSiamRPN) before UpdateNet, and that UpdateNet's contribution is to *replace* that fixed linear rule with a learned one. — [Learning the Model Update for Siamese Trackers (UpdateNet), ICCV 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00855)
- BackTrack (2023) argues that the core risk is not just "how much" but "at what time": "inadequate quality of new templates or inappropriate timing of updates may induce a model drift problem, which severely degrades tracking performance." Its safeguard is a **cycle-consistency check**: after the base tracker follows the target for N frames (e.g., every 15 frames) to produce a candidate template, BackTrack tracks the object *backward* over the same N frames from that candidate; if the backward trajectory doesn't closely overlap (via IoU) with the forward trajectory — especially failing to return to the original starting box — the candidate is rejected as likely a distractor jump. — [BackTrack: Robust template update via Backward Tracking of candidate template (2023)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.10604)
- Drift-correction literature outside deep Siamese trackers (classical template tracking) documents the same two safeguards independently: periodic re-matching against a reference every N frames to "rectify local alignment errors," and refreshing features "only when the target geometry remains stable for a certain number of consecutive frames" (temporal-consistency gating). — [Robust template tracking with drift correction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167865507001018); [general drift-correction summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/304408369_A_New_Template_Tracking_Algorithms_Based_on_Active_Drift_Correction)

### Inferences
- There is a consistent two-layer design across STARK, MixFormer, and BackTrack: (1) a confidence/consistency **gate** that decides if a candidate is even eligible, and (2) an **interval/batching** mechanism (update only every N frames, and among candidates in that window pick the single best) that further suppresses noisy per-frame fluctuations. A project doing per-candidate-crop EMA updates without either safeguard is missing both layers that the strongest published trackers consider necessary.
- The field treats "never update" and "update every frame unconditionally" as the two known failure extremes; the aero_eyes project's default-disabled dynamic prototype sits closer to the naive/no-safeguard end of this spectrum unless it already has both a strong confidence gate and an interval/batching mechanism.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve STARK's exact IoU/label thresholds used to *train* the score head (positive/negative supervision criterion) — the primary PDF was not machine-readable via fetch; only a secondary source's reported operating values (τ=0.5, interval=200) were found, and these should be treated as reported-by-a-secondary-source rather than directly quoted from the STARK paper itself.
- Original SiamRPN/SiamRPN++ papers' own template-update sections were not directly retrieved (SiamRPN++ generally does not update templates at all); could not confirm whether any official SiamRPN variant paper discusses per-frame update policy in detail.

## Key Question 2: What update RATE/strength is typical (EMA with small alpha vs. hard replacement vs. fixed-size memory bank + re-fusion)? Is there a documented sweet spot, or does it depend on the gating mechanism's reliability?

### Takeaway
Three regimes appear in the literature — (1) fixed-coefficient linear/EMA fusion (the historical default, e.g., in SiamFC-style trackers), (2) learned/adaptive fusion coefficients (UpdateNet, "Dynamic Learning Rate of Template Update"), and (3) discrete interval-based hard replacement of a *secondary* dynamic template while keeping a static template frozen (STARK, MixFormer) — and the literature's clear preference in recent (2021+) transformer trackers is regime 3 combined with strict gating, not a continuously-blended running average of the primary template. No paper found reports a universal numeric "sweet spot" alpha; instead, papers argue the correct strength is contingent on how trustworthy the gate is, which is why several works (UpdateNet, "Dynamic Learning Rate of Template Update") replace a fixed alpha with a *learned, input-dependent* one.

### Cited Findings
- UpdateNet's central thesis is that a **fixed linear/EMA coefficient is too simple** and "limits the potential gain" — they replace it with a small CNN that predicts, per-frame, the optimal fused template from (initial template, accumulated template, current-frame template), rather than using a constant blend rate. This is a **trained update network**, not a training-free running average. — [Learning the Model Update for Siamese Trackers (UpdateNet)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00855)
- UpdateNet reports concrete gains from moving off a fixed-rate scheme: +3.9% absolute success score over standard DaSiamRPN linear update on TrackingNet. — [Learning the Model Update for Siamese Trackers (UpdateNet)](https://github.com/zhanglichao/updatenet)
- A dedicated paper, "Dynamic Learning Rate of Template Update for Visual Target Tracking" (MDPI, 2023), exists specifically to argue that the *update learning rate itself* should be adaptive rather than fixed — corroborating that a single constant alpha is considered a known weakness in the field. (Only the title/topic was retrieved; full methodology not fetched.) — [Dynamic Learning Rate of Template Update for Visual Target Tracking](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/11/9/1988)
- STARK/MixFormer's design implicitly rejects continuous EMA blending for the dynamic template component: they perform **hard replacement at discrete checkpoints** (every 200 frames) of a *separate* dynamic-template slot, while a **static/initial template is never touched** — i.e., strength is modulated not by a blend coefficient but by (a) how rarely the update fires and (b) keeping an untouched anchor template as a permanent counterweight. — [MixFormer (CVPR 2022)](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Cui_MixFormer_End-to-End_Tracking_With_Iterative_Mixed_Attention_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf); [STARK](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.17154)
- A dual-template design explicitly separates "static" (frozen, from initial reference) and "dynamic" (updated in real time) templates and fuses them at inference — rather than collapsing everything into one running-average vector — as its drift-mitigation mechanism. — [Frontiers: Siamese tracker with "dynamic–static" dual-template fusion](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurorobotics/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2022.1094892/full)
- General EMA theory (outside tracking specifically) confirms the intuitive tradeoff that motivates all of the above: "the higher the alpha, the more responsive... but... alpha at its extreme value of 0.99 essentially clings to the most recent data point and neglects the older ones" — i.e., high alpha (fast update) sacrifices stability for responsiveness, and low alpha sacrifices responsiveness for stability, which is exactly the drift-vs-staleness tradeoff practitioners must tune. — [Exponential Moving Average on Streaming Data](https://nestedsoftware.com/2018/04/04/exponential-moving-average-on-streaming-data-4hhl.24876.html)

### Inferences
- No paper found states a universal "sweet spot" numeric alpha (e.g., "0.01 is best") — this is consistent with the literature's overall conclusion that the correct update strength is **not a fixed constant** but should depend on (a) how good the gate is and (b) whether a frozen anchor template is retained as insurance. This directly supports treating "gate reliability" and "update rate" as coupled design choices, not independent ones — a weak gate demands a much smaller alpha (or hard-replacement-at-long-intervals) to bound worst-case damage, while a strong/trained gate (like STARK's score head) can tolerate more aggressive hard replacement.
- The shift away from continuous EMA toward "frozen anchor + separately-updated slot, hard-replaced only at long intervals" in the newer (2021–2023) transformer-tracker literature (STARK, MixFormer) suggests this has become the more trusted pattern than pure single-vector EMA blending, likely because it makes the drift bound explicit and inspectable (the anchor never moves) rather than implicit in a decay constant.

### Gaps
- Did not find a controlled ablation study directly comparing EMA-blend-only vs. hard-replace-at-interval vs. learned-update-network on the *same* tracker/benchmark holding the gating mechanism fixed — so the relative ranking of these three regimes, isolated from confounding gate-quality differences, is not confirmed by a single controlled source.
- Could not retrieve the specific alpha values used by SiamFC/DaSiamRPN's original linear-update baseline (referenced by UpdateNet) — the exact historical constant was not found in the sources retrieved.

## Key Question 3: What confidence-gating mechanisms does the literature use to decide a new detection is "safe" to fold into the template/prototype, and how do they compare?

### Takeaway
The literature converges on four largely-complementary gating families — (1) a **learned confidence/reliability score** (STARK's score head, MixFormer's SPM) trained with supervision on whether a candidate is a true positive; (2) **temporal/geometric consistency** (requiring stable target geometry over consecutive frames, or the fixed-interval "best-of-window" selection in MixFormer); (3) **cycle-consistency / backward-verification** (BackTrack); and (4) generative/synthesis-based smoothing (a generative model synthesizing a template from several recent search regions rather than trusting any single raw crop). None of the specific sources retrieved uses a margin-over-second-best gate by name in the tracking literature — that gating style appears more in the pseudo-labeling/self-training literature (see below) — but cycle-consistency (BackTrack) is presented as notably stronger than plain confidence thresholding because it directly tests whether the candidate's own trajectory is self-consistent, rather than relying on a possibly-miscalibrated single confidence score.

### Cited Findings
- **Learned confidence/score-head gating**: STARK's score head is trained to "judge the validity of the dynamic template," and ablating it causes a significant performance drop — direct evidence this class of gate materially prevents drift versus no gate at all. — [STARK](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.17154); [EURASIP secondary source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13634-022-00954-4)
- MixFormer's SPM architecture: a learnable "score token" attends first to the search-region tokens (mined target info), then attends to the initial target token (implicit comparison against the original reference), before an MLP + sigmoid produces the final confidence score — i.e., the gate is explicitly designed to compare the candidate **against the original template**, not just judge it in isolation. This is a form of built-in "distance-from-original-reference" gating baked into the confidence score itself. — [MixFormer (CVPR 2022)](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Cui_MixFormer_End-to-End_Tracking_With_Iterative_Mixed_Attention_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf)
- **Cycle/backward-consistency gating**: BackTrack rejects a candidate template unless its backward-tracked trajectory (over the same N-frame window) overlaps (via IoU) with the original forward trajectory, "as a tracker that jumped to a distractor is unlikely to return to the original target's starting position when tracked in reverse" — a fundamentally different, trajectory-level signal rather than a single-frame confidence number, and one the paper positions as being able to catch drift that a naive confidence threshold would miss (since a confidently-wrong distractor detection can still have high raw appearance-similarity confidence). — [BackTrack](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.10604)
- **Temporal-consistency / stability gating**: classical drift-correction template tracking prevents contamination by "refreshing visual features only when the target geometry remains stable for a certain number of consecutive frames" — i.e., an N-consecutive-confirmations style gate, independent of any deep-learned confidence score. — [Drift-correcting template update strategy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0262885610000144) (via secondary summary)
- **Generative smoothing as an implicit gate**: rather than trusting any single raw candidate crop, a generative-model-based update method "produces a synthetic template from the object search regions of several previous frames" — averaging out single-frame noise/false-positive risk by construction rather than by an explicit accept/reject threshold. — [Generative Target Update for Adaptive Siamese Tracking](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.09938)
- **Diversity + similarity-floor gating for memory writes**: one multi-template memory approach only writes a candidate into memory if it is "diverse enough to represent the object but also similar enough to the base template to avoid drifting to distractor objects" — i.e., a dual-sided gate (upper bound via diversity requirement, lower bound via similarity-to-base requirement) rather than a single confidence threshold. — [Object Tracking with Multiple Dynamic Templates Updating](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-25825-1_11)
- **Cross-domain corroboration from pseudo-labeling/self-training literature**: confidence-threshold gating alone is explicitly flagged as failure-prone because "the model's predicted confidence may not always reflect the true likelihood of the prediction being correct due to issues with confidence calibration," and repeated self-training on the model's own high-confidence-but-wrong predictions causes "confirmation bias (also referred to as self-poisoning)" — the general ML analogue of prototype/template contamination. The mitigation broadly recommended there is **dynamic/adaptive thresholding** rather than a single static confidence cutoff. — [Review of Pseudo Labeling for Semi-Supervised Learning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.07221); [CW-BASS: Confidence-Weighted Boundary-Aware Learning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.15152)

### Inferences
- Comparing across the four gating families: single-frame confidence-score gating (STARK/MixFormer style) is cheapest but, per the pseudo-labeling literature's confirmation-bias warning, is vulnerable to confidently-wrong detections precisely because it relies on the same embedding space's own confidence estimate — the failure mode aero_eyes is worried about (a false positive crop that looks confidently similar) is exactly what a pure appearance-similarity gate cannot catch, since a distractor that resembles the target *will* score high similarity by construction. Cycle-consistency (BackTrack) and multi-frame/temporal-stability gating are structurally more robust to this specific failure mode because they require corroborating evidence beyond a single embedding comparison (i.e., trajectory self-consistency, or persistence across multiple frames) — this is likely the more relevant lesson for a system whose gate is literally "cosine similarity to the current prototype," which is the same signal being used for both matching and gating and therefore cannot self-certify against confidently-wrong matches.
- MixFormer's design detail of comparing the candidate against the **original** template inside the gate (not just judging it in isolation) is a directly transferable idea: a gate that requires high similarity to the *original static reference* as well as to the current (possibly-drifted) prototype would guard against slow accumulated drift in a way that pure per-step confidence cannot.

### Gaps
- Did not find a tracking-specific paper using literal "margin over second-best candidate" as its named gating mechanism (this concept is well established in general open-set/verification literature but a direct SOT-template-update paper using this exact framing was not located in this search pass — flagged as a gap rather than assumed absent).
- Did not find a direct head-to-head quantitative benchmark comparing confidence-threshold gating vs. cycle-consistency gating vs. temporal-consistency gating on identical trackers/sequences; BackTrack's own paper claims state-of-the-art results but a controlled ablation isolating gate type specifically (holding tracker and update-rate fixed) was not retrieved.

## Key Question 4: Do memory-bank/multi-template approaches (K best/diverse templates) outperform single running-average updates, and at what complexity cost?

### Takeaway
The literature generally treats multi-template/memory-bank approaches as a real improvement over single-vector running-average updates specifically because they let the system retain diversity (multiple valid appearances) instead of collapsing everything into one blurred average, but this comes with concrete extra costs: more memory, more compute per query (matching against multiple templates or fusing via attention/retrieval), and additional hyperparameters (memory size, diversity vs. fidelity criteria) that must themselves be tuned or learned.

### Cited Findings
- One multi-template method explicitly motivates itself against single-template collapse: templates are retained "if they are diverse enough to represent the object but also similar enough to the base template to avoid drifting to distractor objects," using a diversity measure across stored templates plus a similarity-to-base lower bound — implying that without this multi-template diversity mechanism, a single fused template would either lose important appearance variation or drift toward distractors. — [Object Tracking with Multiple Dynamic Templates Updating](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-25825-1_11)
- A determinantal point process (DPP) is used by one approach to adaptively select the most diverse subset of candidate templates to store — a principled combinatorial-diversity method rather than simple recency-based memory, indicating the field treats "which K templates to keep" as a nontrivial selection problem, not just "keep the last K." — [Object Tracking with Multiple Dynamic Templates Updating](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-25825-1_11)
- A concrete memory-bank sizing example: "using an 8/8/3 memory configuration with similarity-based retrieval balances capacity and selectivity, large enough to remember long-term appearance changes yet compact enough to avoid redundant noise, while the retrieval step ensures that only the most pertinent memories influence the current template" — i.e., explicit evidence that (a) memory size is a tunable capacity/noise tradeoff, and (b) a **retrieval/gating step at query time** (not just at write time) is used to prevent stale or irrelevant memory entries from corrupting the current match. — [source discussing multi-template memory sizing](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/volumearticle/75765) (exact configuration attribution should be treated as approximate; primary paper not independently re-verified)
- MixFormer's SPM keeps a **window of N (candidate, score) pairs** and picks only the single best at the end of the interval — a lightweight, bounded form of memory-bank thinking (a small rolling buffer + best-of-selection) that avoids the full complexity of a large persistent memory bank while still avoiding the "immediately trust the newest frame" failure mode of naive EMA. — [MixFormer (CVPR 2022)](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Cui_MixFormer_End-to-End_Tracking_With_Iterative_Mixed_Attention_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf)
- Complexity/cost tradeoff stated directly: "more templates mean more computation, so memory length is limited, and a writing criterion is designed so that only feature maps satisfying this criterion would be written into memory" — a direct acknowledgment that multi-template designs must bound memory size for compute reasons, and therefore need a write-gate (not just unconditional accumulation). — [source on memory-length limiting and write criteria](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/volumearticle/75765)

### Inferences
- The pattern across multiple sources is that memory-bank approaches are not "store everything and average" but "bounded buffer + selective write-gate + selective read/retrieval-gate" — three separate gating decisions (what to write, how much to keep, what to actually use per-query) rather than one. This is meaningfully more complex than a single running-average prototype (which has effectively one gate: whether to fuse a given candidate at all, and one parameter: the fusion rate).
- Given that even MixFormer — a well-resourced, heavily benchmarked transformer tracker — only keeps a small rolling window and a single best-of-window template (not a large persistent memory bank), this suggests that for many practical purposes a small bounded window + best-selection may already capture most of the benefit of full memory-bank approaches at much lower complexity, which is a relevant complexity/benefit data point for a project deciding how much machinery to build.

### Gaps
- Could not find a paper directly reporting a quantitative benchmark number (e.g., success-rate delta) comparing "K-diverse-template memory bank" vs. "single EMA-updated prototype" head-to-head on the same benchmark under matched conditions — the sources found argue for multi-template approaches qualitatively/architecturally rather than with a clean isolated ablation number.
- The "8/8/3 memory configuration" figure was found via a secondary/aggregator-style source (an ebooks.iospress.nl listing) rather than confirmed by directly reading the primary paper's methodology section; treat this specific number as lower-confidence and flag it explicitly if used downstream.
- Could not directly access the few-shot/open-set literature's own explicit "growing support set vs. single averaged prototype" comparative results within the tool-call budget (see Key Question 6 for what was found on open-set prototype approaches generally).

## Key Question 5: Is "template drift"/"prototype drift" a well-documented failure mode, and what are the standard diagnostics/mitigations (periodic reset, capping cumulative drift, similarity bound vs. original)?

### Takeaway
Yes — "template drift" / "model drift" is a named, extensively documented failure mode across both classical and deep-learning tracking literature, consistently attributed to updating on erroneous candidates (background, distractors, occlusions) without adequate gating. The standard, most-cited mitigation across the sources found is **retaining an untouched original/static anchor** (rather than periodically resetting a single evolving prototype back to the original) combined with fusing it against the drifting dynamic component at inference time — functionally similar to "capping drift distance from the original," since the static anchor permanently pulls the effective match signal back toward the original reference.

### Cited Findings
- Direct naming and characterization of the failure mode: "Target drift occurs when SiameseFC trackers suffer from drift over time as the tracker slowly starts focusing on distractive backgrounds rather than the target, and eventually loses the target." — [PMC: Robust Template Adjustment Siamese Network](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7923413/)
- Also named "model drift": "inadequate quality of new templates or inappropriate timing of updates may induce a model drift problem, which severely degrades tracking performance." — [BackTrack](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.10604)
- **Dual/static+dynamic template fusion as the standard mitigation** (functionally: never fully replace the original, always keep it in the fused decision): "A Siamese tracker with 'dynamic–static' dual-template fusion and dynamic template adaptive update combines a static template and a dynamic template that is updated in real time for object tracking." — [Frontiers: dynamic-static dual-template fusion tracker](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurorobotics/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2022.1094892/full)
- STARK/MixFormer implement the same principle architecturally: the **static/initial template branch is never updated**, only a separate dynamic-template slot is refreshed (and only when gated) — meaning the original reference always remains a live, undiluted input to the final matching decision, rather than the system trusting a single vector that has been progressively blended away from the original. — [STARK](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.17154); [MixFormer](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Cui_MixFormer_End-to-End_Tracking_With_Iterative_Mixed_Attention_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf)
- **Periodic re-matching/reset as a classical diagnostic-correction technique** (pre-deep-learning template tracking): "Periodic template matching is performed every N frames to rectify local alignment errors as a drift correction strategy." Also, in a different (non-tracking, but structurally analogous) streaming/recurrent-state context: "periodically reset the recurrent state... every N frames... However, while resets can reduce within-segment drift, they also discard accumulated scene context and risk introducing scale discontinuities at reset boundaries" — i.e., periodic hard reset is a documented technique but comes with its own explicitly noted cost (loss of legitimately-accumulated adaptation, and discontinuities at reset points). — [Robust template tracking with drift correction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167865507001018); [reset-strategy tradeoff discussion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.18493)
- **Generative synthesis as an alternative to both hard reset and raw-crop accumulation**: producing a synthetic template from multiple recent search regions (rather than either a single fused running-average vector or the single latest raw detection) as a way to stabilize the update against any individual frame's error. — [Generative Target Update for Adaptive Siamese Tracking](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.09938)

### Inferences
- No source explicitly frames the mitigation as "cap the cosine-distance the prototype is allowed to move from the original" as a *numeric* bound — but the static+dynamic dual-template pattern (STARK, MixFormer, the Frontiers dual-template tracker) is the field's de facto implementation of that same idea: instead of enforcing a numeric similarity-bound constraint on a single evolving vector, they sidestep the problem by keeping two vectors (one frozen, one adaptive) and letting the *matching/fusion function itself* enforce that the original is always weighed in. This is arguably a more robust engineering pattern than a manually-tuned drift-distance cap, since it doesn't require picking a threshold at all — it's structurally guaranteed the original always has a vote.
- The explicitly noted cost of periodic full reset (discarding legitimately accumulated adaptation, and discontinuities at reset boundaries) suggests that if aero_eyes considers a "reset to original periodically" safeguard, a **soft weighted fusion against the frozen original** (dual-template style) is likely preferable to a **hard periodic reset**, per the tradeoff explicitly flagged in the reset-strategy source.

### Gaps
- Did not find a tracking paper that names and evaluates a specific "maximum cumulative drift distance / similarity-bound-from-original" constraint as an explicit, tunable numeric hyperparameter (as opposed to the static-anchor architectural workaround described above). This may be a genuine gap in the literature (the dual-template pattern seems to have become the preferred solution over an explicit distance-cap constraint), or may simply not have surfaced in this search pass — flagged as uncertain rather than confirmed absent.

## Key Question 6: Does the open-set/incremental few-shot recognition literature have a different/complementary perspective on prototype update, and does it suggest different safeguards than tracking?

### Takeaway
The few-shot class-incremental learning (FSCIL) / incremental few-shot literature frames the analogous problem as "prototype drift" caused by concept/data-distribution drift across incremental stages, and its primary complementary contribution (relative to tracking) is a stronger emphasis on **not forgetting old/base classes while incorporating new ones** (catastrophic forgetting) and on **calibrating** prototypes computed from very few samples (variance/bias correction) — safeguards oriented around cross-class interference and statistical prototype quality rather than tracking's frame-to-frame appearance-drift and false-positive-contamination framing. Some methods explicitly favor **training-free prototype calibration** and **replay of stored prototypes** as safeguards, which is conceptually adjacent to the tracking literature's "keep frozen original + fuse" pattern but arrived at from a different angle (preventing forgetting of prior knowledge rather than preventing appearance drift).

### Cited Findings
- Direct statement of prototype drift as a named issue in this literature: "prototype-based methods face challenges in [incremental learning] scenarios due to concept drift or data distribution drift, making prototype samples fail to accurately represent the latest data distribution, and newly introduced later-task classes might differ conceptually from earlier classes, causing conflicts within the prototype space." — [via search synthesis of FSCIL survey material](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.08130)
- FSCIL's defining dual challenge, distinct from tracking's single-object drift framing: "When faced with novel few-shot tasks in each incremental stage, systems must account for both catastrophic forgetting of old knowledge and overfitting of new categories with limited training data." — [A survey on few-shot class-incremental learning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.08130)
- A **training-free calibration** approach exists specifically to correct/stabilize prototypes computed from few samples without requiring gradient-based retraining — directly relevant to aero_eyes' constraint of staying in a frozen embedding space, since it is a *post-hoc correction of which vectors get fused/how*, not a retrained update network. — [Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning via Training-Free Prototype Calibration](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.05229)
- **Prototype replay** as a forgetting-safeguard: the Efficient Prototype Replay and Calibration (EPRC) method "incorporate[s] nonlinear transformation functions into similarity computation to implicitly calibrate generated prototypes, and replay stored prototypes to relieve catastrophic forgetting while rectifying prototypes to be more discriminative in the incremental-training stage" — i.e., old prototypes are explicitly retained and periodically reintroduced ("replayed") rather than allowed to be fully overwritten by newly-computed ones, which is conceptually parallel to tracking's frozen-anchor-template pattern but motivated by preventing forgetting of prior *classes* rather than preventing drift of a single object's appearance. — [Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning via Efficient Prototype Replay and Calibration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10217101/)
- Base prototype-network definition, confirming this literature's default starting point is the same single-running-mean prototype construction aero_eyes already uses upfront: "Class prototypes are typically defined as the mean of all samples from a class in the embedding space... classification is performed by computing distances between embedded query samples and class prototypes." — [general FSCIL/prototype-network background, corroborated across multiple sources including the survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.08130)
- Open-set recognition (a closely related but distinct problem — detecting *unknown* classes, not just updating known-class prototypes) has explored "online" prototype maintenance: "some approaches update class prototypes and variance in an online manner to maintain dynamic recognition systems," and recent (2025) work constructs "Unknown Support Prototype Sets" using explicit Support and Diversity metrics — again echoing the tracking literature's diversity-based memory-bank thinking, but for representing the *unknown/negative* space rather than the *positive* target. — [Unknown Support Prototype Set for Open Set Recognition (2025)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11263-025-02384-9)

### Inferences
- The FSCIL/open-set literature's central safeguard against prototype corruption is fundamentally about **inter-class interference and forgetting** (does updating prototype B corrupt the boundary with prototype A, and does the system forget A's original prototype entirely), which is a different threat model than aero_eyes' single-target-tracking concern (does updating the one target's prototype toward a false-positive crop pull it toward background/other-objects). This means the FSCIL safeguards (replay of stored prototypes, calibration for few-sample bias/variance) are only partially transferable — the "replay stored prototypes, don't fully overwrite" idea is directly transferable and reinforces the tracking literature's frozen-anchor pattern from an independent direction, but FSCIL's forgetting-focused calibration techniques (which are about correcting few-shot sampling bias/variance in mean estimation) address a different failure mode than false-positive contamination.
- The convergence of two independent literatures (SOT tracking's "keep static anchor + fuse" and FSCIL's "replay stored/original prototypes, don't fully overwrite") on structurally the same mitigating pattern — never letting the running/updated representation fully replace the original — is a meaningfully strong signal that aero_eyes' own mechanism should, at minimum, retain the original 3-reference-photo prototype as a permanent, never-overwritten anchor that participates in every match decision (e.g., via fusion or as a floor/regularizer), rather than allowing the online update to progressively become the sole prototype.

### Gaps
- Could not find an FSCIL/open-set paper that frames its safeguard in terms directly analogous to tracking's "confidence gating of individual samples before fusion" (margin, temporal consistency, etc.) — this literature seems to operate more at the level of whole-episode/whole-session calibration and replay rather than per-sample accept/reject gating decisions, so a direct comparison of gating mechanism *types* between the two literatures is only partially answerable from what was found; recommend treating the SOT tracking literature (Key Questions 1–5 above) as the primary source for per-candidate gating-mechanism design, and the FSCIL literature primarily as corroboration for the "retain/replay the original, don't fully overwrite" principle.
- Did not locate a direct, controlled comparison within the open-set/FSCIL literature of "single running-average/replayed prototype" vs. "growing support set of individual exemplars" — only qualitative descriptions of both approaches existing in the field were found, not a head-to-head performance comparison.

## Overall Cross-Cutting Note for the Report Writer

Across both literatures (SOT tracking and FSCIL/open-set), the single most consistent, well-evidenced pattern relevant to aero_eyes' training-free, frozen-embedding-space constraint is: **never let the online-updated prototype fully replace/overwrite the original reference representation** — instead keep the original as a permanent, undiluted anchor (dual static/dynamic template in STARK/MixFormer/the Frontiers tracker; prototype replay in FSCIL) and gate any candidate that's allowed to influence the *adaptive* component with more than a single raw appearance-similarity score (STARK/MixFormer's learned score head, BackTrack's cycle-consistency check, or classical temporal-consistency-over-N-frames gating) — because a pure cosine-similarity gate is structurally unable to catch a confidently-wrong distractor, since that's exactly the signal being used for both matching and gating. UpdateNet is the one prominent technique found that requires training a dedicated update network (a CNN taking initial/accumulated/current templates as input) rather than a training-free scheme, and should be flagged as the heavier-lift option if cited.
