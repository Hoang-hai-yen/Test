# Adapt the encoder, don't replace it

The research converges on a counterintuitive conclusion: the ~0.335–0.38 TP/FP separation ceiling this project measures on its own drone footage is very unlikely to be an encoder problem, because DINOv3 — already in the project's candidate stack — is the current best-in-class general-purpose instance-retrieval encoder as of its August 2025 release, with no newer challenger found that surpasses it on arbitrary-instance matching. What the literature identifies as missing is not a better frozen backbone but a **thin, trainable adaptation layer** that sits on top of the frozen embeddings and is explicitly trained to close the clean-reference-vs-degraded-crop domain gap — a well-studied problem in adjacent fields (cross-resolution re-ID, aerial-ground re-ID, degradation-robust animal re-ID) that has simply not yet been applied, in published form, to arbitrary-object instance verification. This means a pure off-the-shelf swap (trying yet another frozen encoder) is a low-expected-value move, while a full heavy retrain of a dedicated architecture is likely infeasible given only 3 reference images per instance and no category-specific training data at inference time. The evidence instead points to a middle path: a small, cheap fine-tuning investment in a lightweight adapter or degradation-augmentation step layered onto the existing frozen encoder, escalating to fuller fine-tuning only if the project accumulates enough labeled positive/negative crop pairs to justify it. The remainder of this report lays out why the encoder itself is not the lever to pull, what specifically causes the domain-gap ceiling, and a concrete, effort-ranked comparison of four candidate interventions.

## The encoder is not the bottleneck — no better frozen backbone exists to swap in

Three 2025 benchmarking efforts converge on DINOv3 as the reference best-in-class general-purpose encoder for instance-level (not category-level) retrieval, and none of them identify a superior alternative for arbitrary physical objects. DINOv3 (Meta, arXiv:2508.10104) reports improving over DINOv2 "by +10.8 points on Met and +7.6 points on AmsterTime," with "+10.9 GAP improvement" specifically on instance retrieval tasks, evaluated with plain cosine similarity on the CLS token ([DINOv3 paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.10104)). ILIAS, a 2025 benchmark built specifically to stress-test instance-level retrieval against 100M distractors, found that "models fine-tuned on specific domains, such as landmarks or products, excel in that domain but fail on ILIAS" — meaning domain-specific fine-tuning does not transfer to arbitrary-object instance retrieval, but it also found no raw encoder superior to the DINO/CLIP-family foundation models it tested; its only reported improvement lever was "a linear adaptation layer using multi-domain class supervision," layered on top of the frozen encoder rather than replacing it ([ILIAS paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11748)). A March 2026 Visual Product Search Benchmark is actively re-litigating DINO-vs-CLIP-vs-proprietary-multimodal for exact-instance product retrieval, which confirms the question is still open in principle, but no source in this research surfaced a specific challenger encoder with published numbers beating DINOv3 on arbitrary-instance matching ([arXiv:2603.17186](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17186)). Since the project's own baseline already spans DINOv2, DINOv3, CLIP, SigLIP, FG-CLIP, and RADIO, it has effectively already run the "try a different frozen encoder" experiment across the field's strongest available options — which is strong indirect evidence that another encoder swap will not move the TP/FP separation ceiling by much.

The literature also explains *why* no swap will help: there is no published, purpose-built, sub-50M-parameter, arbitrary-object instance-verification head evaluated for exactly this scenario (clean references vs. compressed/degraded candidate crops). This is described as "a genuine literature gap rather than a search failure," corroborated by the VICP paper's own framing that "most re-identification research has focused on humans and vehicles" and that existing methods "do not generalize to diverse and long-tail objects" ([VICP, arXiv:2508.21222](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21222)). The few-shot counting literature this project already draws on (its own commit history shows DAVE-style verification already applied) offers the closest analog: DAVE's "detect-and-verify" paradigm clusters the detector's own internal appearance features and applies a relative-size threshold to reject outliers, explicitly to "identify and remove the outliers" among same-image detections — but this is an intra-image outlier-rejection mechanism tuned for counting, not a cross-domain, reference-vs-video-crop identity verifier, and no source found benchmarks it (or GeCo/GeCo2's dense object-query embeddings) as a stand-alone instance-identity verifier against a separately trained encoder baseline ([DAVE paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16622); [GeCo2 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08048)). The practical takeaway is that the field's actual recipe for arbitrary-instance matching is "frozen foundation encoder plus a thin, separately trained adaptation/verification layer" — never "a different frozen encoder alone" — and this project's own architecture (DAVE-derived verification bolted onto foundation-model cosine similarity) is already structurally aligned with that recipe; what it is missing is a layer trained specifically to close the domain-quality gap rather than to reject same-image outliers.

## The domain gap has a name, and cheap, validated fixes exist for it

The specific failure mode described in the assignment — clean, crisp close-up reference photos matched against compressed, motion-blurred, lower-resolution, differently-scaled drone-video crops — maps directly onto an established sub-field called Cross-Resolution Re-ID (CR-ReID), and separately onto degradation-robust re-identification research, both of which report concrete, low-data techniques that outperform doing nothing. The CR-ReID field has explicitly moved away from super-resolution preprocessing because "SR models cannot completely compensate for the missing information in LR images," instead favoring lightweight, post-hoc, frozen-backbone feature-alignment modules ([Remote Sensing 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs16020278)). The clearest example is Vector-Panning Feature Alignment (VPFA): a lightweight 3-layer MLP with residual connections and a Tanh gate, applied purely post-hoc on top of a frozen backbone's features with **no backbone retraining required**, built on the observation that resolution-induced feature discrepancy follows "a consistent, resolution-related semantic direction in the embedding space." VPFA outperformed SR-based baselines on MLR-Market-1501 (94.1% vs 91.5% Rank-1) and on the real-world CAVIAR dataset (66.6% vs 63.6% Rank-1) ([arXiv:2510.00936](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.00936)).

A second, independently converging line of evidence comes from degradation-robust animal re-ID, which studies exactly the asymmetric scenario this project faces: a "corrupted-query" protocol where "synthetic corruptions [are applied] only to query images while gallery images remain unchanged," explicitly designed to reflect "practical...deployments where a degraded field image is used as the query and matched against original reference images in the gallery" ([DARA, arXiv:2607.16644](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16644)) — structurally identical to matching a degraded drone crop against clean reference photos. A dedicated 2026 study built progressively realistic degradation pipelines (motion/defocus/Gaussian blur combined with multi-factor downscaling, JPEG compression, and noise) and found the most diverse pipeline yielded an **8.5% Rank-1 accuracy gain** on real low-clarity images over an unaugmented baseline, critically confirming that "diverse" degradation composition beats naive single-factor augmentation (e.g., downsampling alone) and that this approach requires no additional labeled data beyond the existing training split, generalizing to individuals unseen during training with only a 2–3% accuracy drop ([arXiv:2603.04163](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.04163)). Layered on top of this, the DARA method trains a lightweight low-rank residual adapter plus a small router on a frozen backbone, using only synthetic ImageNet-C-style corruptions (no corruption-type labels, no target-category-specific dataset) and reports a clean, ranked comparison across four re-ID datasets: standard fine-tuning reaches 30.17% mAP, generic AugMix augmentation reaches 41.67%, DARA's lightweight adapter reaches 55.70%, and full corrupted-data fine-tuning tops out at 63.35% — DARA captures roughly 77% of the full-fine-tuning gain at only **0.49% extra parameters and 0.05% extra FLOPs** (0.23ms added latency), and it generalizes across datasets and to unseen corruption types ([DARA, arXiv:2607.16644](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16644)). Together, these two independent research threads (CR-ReID's VPFA and animal-reID's DARA) triangulate on the same architectural pattern the ILIAS paper separately recommended: keep the strong frozen encoder, add a small trained module that specifically absorbs the reference-vs-degraded-query gap.

## Altitude and viewpoint compound the quality gap and add their own lightweight fixes

Because the project's domain gap is aerial/drone-specific, not just a resolution gap, the aerial-ground re-ID literature adds a second, independent axis of evidence. The AG-VPReID 2025 Challenge, using drones at 80–120m altitude, found methods experienced a **4–6% Rank-1 accuracy drop** purely from altitude increase, and that "ground-to-aerial matching consistently outperformed aerial-to-ground matching," meaning the direction of the domain gap (crisp reference → degraded aerial query, as in this project) is the harder direction, not an arbitrary symmetric problem ([arXiv:2506.22843](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.22843v1)). Notably, the winning 2025 challenge solutions did not rely on heavier backbones or larger training sets to win — the top two methods (71.56% and 71.89% Rank-1) differed by less than half a point despite one using a much larger EVA-CLIP-L-14 backbone with more training data, while the top approach instead used a modified CLIP backbone with lightweight, cheap interventions: attention-based frame pooling, soft-biometric attribute filtering to pre-reject incompatible gallery candidates, and simple flip-augmentation with embedding averaging ([arXiv:2506.22843](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.22843v1)). A separate drone-footage-specific paper found that attribute-guided pre-filtering (using robust coarse attributes that survive downsampling) improved Rank-1 by **+6.59 points at 16% resolution and +5.55 points at 9% resolution** on Market-1501, using existing attribute annotations rather than new large-scale labeling ([PMC11946598](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11946598/)). No source found isolates video-codec compression (H.264/H.265 blocking, as opposed to generic JPEG or resolution loss) as a controlled variable, and no non-person aerial-instance benchmark exists — both are genuine gaps rather than a search failure — but the consistent finding across every aerial-ReID source is that *marginal, cheap adaptations on a strong frozen backbone beat scaling up the backbone or the training set*, reinforcing the same conclusion drawn from the CR-ReID literature above.

## Four concrete options, ranked by effort and risk

Given that the encoder is not the lever and the gap is well-characterized, the following four options represent the most promising concrete interventions surfaced by this research, ordered from cheapest to most expensive.

| Option | What it is | Expected effort | Main risk / tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Reference-side degradation augmentation (no model change)** | Synthetically degrade the 3 clean reference images (blur + downsample + JPEG-style compression, "diverse" not single-factor) to generate multiple degraded variants, then re-run existing cosine-similarity matching against this expanded, quality-matched reference set | Very low — days; no training, reuses existing pipeline | No source directly ablates *only* degrading the reference index while keeping the matching function frozen (all found evidence degrades data at *training* time for a learned adapter/backbone); effect size on this project's data is therefore uncertain and should be validated empirically as a free first experiment, not assumed to fully close the gap |
| **2. DARA-style lightweight low-rank residual adapter on frozen DINOv3** | Freeze DINOv3, train a small low-rank adapter + router using synthetic ImageNet-C-style corruptions of available images (references, plus any clean video frames) — no paired HR/LR data collection needed | Medium — roughly 1–3 weeks to implement the adapter/router, build the synthetic corruption pipeline, and train | Published results (55.70% vs 63.35% mAP vs full fine-tuning) are from animal re-ID benchmarks (cattle, tiger), not drone video or generic rigid objects; needs direct validation on this project's own TP/FP footage before trusting the ~77%-of-full-fine-tuning-gain figure to transfer, but the near-zero parameter/latency cost (0.49% params, 0.05% FLOPs) makes a failed experiment cheap to discard |
| **3. VPFA-style post-hoc feature-alignment MLP on frozen embeddings** | A tiny 3-layer MLP with residual connection and gating, trained to "pan" degraded-crop embeddings back toward the reference-embedding direction in feature space, no backbone retraining | Medium — comparable implementation effort to option 2, but training requires many HR/LR paired examples (the MLR-ReID protocol downsamples large labeled datasets to generate pairs); with only 3 references per instance, generating enough synthetic pairs to train this reliably is a real constraint | Validated only on person cross-resolution re-ID (Market-1501/CUHK03/CAVIAR), not on cross-domain compression/blur or arbitrary object categories; resolution is a narrower slice of this project's actual degradation profile (which also includes motion blur, codec compression, and altitude/viewpoint change), so it may only partially address the observed gap |
| **4. Full lightweight Siamese/triplet fine-tune (OSNet-scale, ~2.2M params) on the project's own labeled crop pairs** | Retrain a small ReID-native architecture (e.g., OSNet via Torchreid, which ships complete triplet-loss training code) end-to-end on positive/negative crop pairs mined from the project's own drone footage, with hard-negative mining | High — several weeks to months; requires accumulating and labeling a meaningful volume of positive/negative crop pairs beyond the 3 references per instance, plus a training/evaluation loop | Architecture transfer across categories is real but uneven and method-dependent — OpenAnimals found "some techniques generalize well, many do not" when person-ReID architectures were retrained on a new domain, so success is not guaranteed even with adequate data ([arXiv:2410.00204](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00204)); no source gives a validated minimum pair-count for this exact scenario, so the required data volume is an engineering estimate, not a benchmarked figure — highest potential payoff but highest cost and least certain return |

Two options considered and explicitly not recommended: a pure off-the-shelf encoder swap (e.g., trying yet another frozen foundation model, or a packaged tool like Towhee's ISC operator or fastdup) is unlikely to help because the project has already exhausted the strongest available frozen encoders and no evidence surfaced of a superior challenger; and VICP's LLM-guided visual-prompting approach, while the closest published framing of this exact problem, was excluded because no parameter count or inference-latency figure could be found and an LLM-in-the-loop design is very likely too heavy for near-real-time per-crop verification, making it a research direction to watch rather than a near-term option.

## Recommendation: a staged fine-tuning investment, not a swap and not a full retrain

The evidence supports neither pole of the "swap vs. fine-tune vs. do nothing" framing cleanly — it supports a **staged, lightweight fine-tuning investment**, sequenced by cost. Start with Option 1 (reference degradation augmentation) as a nearly free validation experiment, since it requires no training and directly tests whether the domain gap — rather than some other failure mode — actually explains the 0.335–0.38 TP/FP separation ceiling; if this alone produces a meaningful jump in separation, it derisks and directly informs the design of the next stage. Proceed to Option 2 (a DARA-style low-rank residual adapter on the already-in-use frozen DINOv3) as the primary investment, because it is the only intervention in this comparison with (a) a directly analogous published benchmark on the exact reference-clean/query-degraded structure this project has, (b) near-zero parameter and latency cost, making a negative result cheap, and (c) no dependency on large labeled datasets — only synthetic corruption of images already on hand. Treat Option 3 (VPFA) as a lower-priority alternative to trial in parallel only if paired training data can be generated cheaply, since its resolution-only focus likely under-covers this project's multi-factor degradation profile (blur, compression, altitude/scale, all combined). Reserve Option 4 (full OSNet-scale Siamese fine-tune) as an escalation path to pursue only if the adapter approach plateaus and the project is willing to invest in building a labeled positive/negative crop-pair pipeline from its own footage — it has the highest theoretical ceiling but the least certain, least data-efficient path to get there, and the OpenAnimals evidence warns explicitly that architecture transfer to a new domain is not guaranteed even with adequate retraining data.

This sequencing also surfaces an important epistemic caveat worth carrying forward: nothing in the literature directly benchmarks this project's precise scenario — three clean references per arbitrary physical object instance, matched against compressed drone-video crops. Every promising technique found (VPFA, DARA, degradation-augmented training, attribute-guided aerial filtering) was validated on adjacent but distinct problems (person/vehicle/animal re-ID, not arbitrary rigid objects like ID cards or life jackets), which is precisely the literature gap that VICP, ISAR, and OSCAR are each independently trying to fill as of 2025–2026. That makes this project's own upcoming experiments — degrading references, training a lightweight adapter, and measuring the resulting TP/FP separation on real drone footage — not merely an engineering exercise but a genuine, currently-unanswered empirical question in the field, and the results are worth treating as potentially publishable or at least reusable evidence beyond this project's immediate needs.
